Foundations you are graded on everywhere

Every report in this course is really an argument about bias, variance and evidence. Get these five ideas exact and the other three units become bookkeeping.

What "learning" means here

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine three ways to learn to recognize dogs. Someone shows you photos labeled "dog" or "not dog" (supervised) — you learn from answers. Someone hands you a pile of random photos with no labels at all, and you sort them into piles that seem to go together (unsupervised) — you learn by noticing patterns. Or you're a puppy bumping into furniture, and the only feedback is "ouch" sometimes, way after you took the wrong turn (reinforcement) — you learn from consequences that show up late.

Mitchell's definition is the one the course leans on: a program learns from experience E with respect to task T and performance measure P if its performance at T, measured by P, improves with E. Every design decision you make in an assignment is a choice of one of those three letters.

Isbell and Littman frame the whole course as generalization, not memorization: induction from examples to a function that behaves on data you have never seen. The three families map cleanly onto what supervision you are given:

UnsupervisedOnly xCompact descriptionWhat structure justifies calling two points "the same"?
ReinforcementStates, actions, delayed scalar rewardPolicyWhich action now, given that the payoff arrives much later?
Say it this way in a report

"Induction is under-determined; the learner's inductive bias is what breaks the tie." That single sentence is the through-line of Unit 1, and graders reward candidates who use it to explain why two algorithms disagree on the same data instead of merely reporting that they do.

Inductive bias: restriction vs preference

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

You're guessing the shape of a hidden toy from a few peeks through a keyhole. Tons of different shapes would fit what you've seen — your inductive bias is your personal guessing rule, the thing that makes you assume "probably a simple, familiar shape" instead of some crazy spiky one nobody's ever seen. Different learners have different guessing rules, which is why they guess different toys from the very same peeks.

A hypothesis space H plus a search order is a bias. Split it in two — the distinction shows up in almost every exam question:

  • Restriction bias — what H can represent at all. Linear regression restricts you to hyperplanes; a depth-1 tree restricts you to axis-aligned single splits.
  • Preference bias — which hypothesis inside H the algorithm reaches first. ID3 prefers shorter trees with high-information-gain attributes near the root; backprop prefers small weights and smooth functions because it starts small and moves by gradient.

Where points get lost

Writing "k-NN has no bias because it is non-parametric." It has a very strong preference bias — the locality/smoothness assumption plus equal feature weighting — and that assumption is exactly what the curse of dimensionality destroys.

Two learners fit the training set perfectly but disagree on a new point. What explains the disagreement?

Only the inductive bias. The data alone cannot distinguish them — both are consistent hypotheses. The difference must come from their restriction bias (one cannot represent what the other does) or preference bias (both could represent both answers, but their search reaches different ones first).

Bias–variance decomposition, and how to read a learning curve

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

Think about throwing darts at a board. Bias is being a bad thrower for a boring, consistent reason — your arm always pulls left, so every dart lands left of the bullseye, again and again. Variance is being a wild thrower — darts land left, right, up, down, all over — you're unpredictable. A model that's too simple is the "always left" thrower; a model that's too complicated is the "wild" thrower — and you need to fix both to hit the middle.

For squared loss with target y = f(x) + ε, Var(ε) = σ², the expected error of a learner trained on random training sets decomposes exactly:

E[(y − ĥ(x))²] = (E[ĥ(x)] − f(x))² + E[(ĥ(x) − E[ĥ(x)])²] + σ²bias²  +  variance  +  irreducible noise

Bias is error from a hypothesis space too small or too rigid to contain the truth — it does not shrink with more data. Variance is sensitivity to which training sample you happened to draw — it does shrink with more data. This is why the two diagnostic curves say different things:

Model-complexity curve (x = complexity)
  • Train error falls monotonically.
  • CV error is U-shaped.
  • Left of the minimum: underfit / high bias.
  • Right of the minimum: overfit / high variance.
Learning curve (x = training-set size)
  • Curves converge high and flat → high bias; more data will not help, change the model.
  • Wide persistent gap, CV still descending → high variance; more data (or regularization) will help.
Assignment 1 tactic

Every SL report wants both curve types per algorithm per dataset. Plot them with error bars over folds or seeds, and write the sentence the plot supports — "the gap does not close by 100% of the data, so this is variance, which is why pruning at depth 6 improves CV by 4 points." Plots without that sentence are what "insufficient analysis" means in the rubric.

Information theory in one page

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

Entropy is a fancy word for "how surprised would I be?" If someone tells you "the sky is blue" — meh, you basically knew that, so that's low entropy, not much real information. If they tell you "it snowed in the Sahara today" — whoa, genuinely surprising, high entropy, real information. A perfectly fair coin flip is the most surprising thing there is, because you truly can't guess it ahead of time.

Entropy is the expected number of bits to encode a draw from P; everything else in the unit is arithmetic on it.

H(X) = − Σx P(x) log₂ P(x)   ·   H(X|Y) = Σy P(y) H(X | Y=y)
I(X;Y) = H(X) − H(X|Y) = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X,Y)Mutual information is symmetric, non-negative, and zero exactly when X ⫫ Y

Two facts worth memorizing: entropy of a binary variable is maximal (1 bit) at p = 0.5 and 0 at p ∈ {0,1}; and KL divergence D(P‖Q) = Σ P(x) log(P(x)/Q(x)) is non-negative, zero iff P = Q, and not symmetric — so it is a divergence, not a distance. KL is the objective behind MIMIC's dependency-tree projection and behind maximum-likelihood fitting generally.

Where points get lost

Calling KL a metric. It fails symmetry and the triangle inequality. Also: information gain is mutual information between the attribute and the label — saying so out loud connects Unit 1 to Unit 3's feature selection and earns credit.

Evaluation you can defend

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine studying for a test using the actual test questions, then bragging about your perfect score — that doesn't prove you learned anything, it proves you memorized the answer key. That's why you never touch the real test set until the very end: you practice and tune on separate practice questions (cross-validation) so your final score on the real test actually means something.

The test set is a one-shot instrument. Every choice you make after looking at it leaks. The defensible pipeline:

split train / test once, stratified, fixed seed tune hyper-parameters by k-fold CV inside train only (k = 5 or 10) refit best config on all of train report once on test — and report the CV variance, not just the mean
  • Stratify when classes are imbalanced, and stop quoting accuracy there: a 95/5 split makes "always negative" look 95% right. Use F1, balanced accuracy, ROC-AUC or PR-AUC, and say which and why.
  • Nested CV (outer loop for estimation, inner for tuning) when you need an unbiased estimate of the whole tuning procedure — worth one sentence in a report even if you only run flat CV.
  • Wall-clock is a first-class result in this course: fit time and query time separate the eager learners (trees, SVM, nets) from the lazy one (k-NN), and the graders look for it.

Supervised learning

Function approximation from labelled examples. Five algorithm families, one theory chapter, and one Bayesian chapter that quietly explains all of them.

Decision trees & ID3

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

It's exactly like the game 20 Questions — you ask the single most useful yes/no question first (the one that narrows things down fastest), then based on the answer, ask the next most useful question, and so on, until you can confidently guess. A decision tree is just that game written down as a flowchart, and "training" the tree means figuring out the best order to ask the questions.

A tree is a sequence of questions; learning one is choosing the order of the questions. ID3 is greedy and top-down: pick the attribute with the highest information gain, split, recurse.

ID3(Examples, Target, Attributes) if all Examples have same label → return leaf(label) if Attributes empty → return leaf(majority label) A ← argmaxa ∈ Attributes Gain(Examples, a) root ← A for each value v of A: Sv ← {e ∈ Examples : e.A = v} if Sv empty → child = leaf(majority label of Examples) else child = ID3(Sv, Target, Attributes − {A}) return root
Gain(S, A) = H(S) − Σv ∈ values(A) (|Sv|/|S|) · H(Sv)= I(label ; A) — the entropy you expect to remove by asking A

The properties you must be able to state

  • Greedy, no backtracking — reaches a locally good tree, not the globally shortest one; finding the optimal tree is NP-hard.
  • Bias: restriction = axis-parallel splits (so an XOR-in-two-continuous-features boundary needs a staircase); preference = short trees, informative attributes at the top.
  • Gain is biased toward many-valued attributes — a unique ID column has maximal gain and zero generalization. Fix with gain ratio (divide by the split's own entropy, C4.5) or Gini impurity.
  • Continuous attributes get thresholded: sort values, consider midpoints where the label changes. An attribute can be reused down a path (unlike discrete ID3, where it is exhausted).
  • Overfitting control: pre-pruning (max depth, min samples per leaf, gain threshold) or post-pruning (grow full, then reduced-error pruning against a validation set, or rule post-pruning). Post-pruning is generally stronger because early stopping can miss a split that only pays off in combination.
Gini(S) = 1 − Σi pi²   vs   H(S) = − Σi pi log₂ piBoth peak at the uniform distribution; Gini is cheaper (no log) and nearly always picks the same split
Where points get lost

Saying deeper trees are "more accurate." Deeper trees have lower training error and higher variance. In a report, the depth-vs-CV-error curve is the evidence; the sentence is "past depth d the tree begins fitting sample-specific noise, and CV error rises while train error goes to zero."

Why can a decision tree represent any boolean function, and what does that cost?

With enough depth you can enumerate every row of the truth table as a root-to-leaf path — so the restriction bias for discrete inputs is essentially none. The cost is size: parity/XOR over n attributes needs O(2ⁿ) nodes because no single attribute has any information gain on its own, so the greedy heuristic is also blind at the root.

Regression & classification: the linear baseline

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

If someone asks "how tall will this kid be as an adult?" that's a number — regression. If they ask "is this kid going to prefer basketball or soccer?" that's a category — classification. Both jobs can use the exact same tool: draw the best straight line through what you know, then either read a number off it (regression) or check which side of the line you land on (classification).

Two questions, one machinery. Regression predicts a continuous y; classification predicts a discrete one — and the cheapest way to build either is a linear function of the inputs, fit by minimizing a loss.

ŷ = w·x + bLinear regression: choose w, b to minimize squared error over the training set

Least squares, two ways

  • Closed form — w* = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy, the normal equations. Exact, but needs XᵀX invertible and costs O(d³) to solve — fine for small d, not for wide feature spaces.
  • Gradient descent — w ← w − α∇wJ(w) on the same objective. Same optimum for a convex loss, but this is the version that generalizes: swap the loss and you get logistic regression below; add layers and it is exactly the update rule Unit 1's neural-net topic calls backprop.
Why this sits between trees and neural nets

Trees search combinatorially over splits. Linear and logistic regression are the two-line version of everything after them: pick a hypothesis class, pick a loss, minimize it continuously over weights instead.

Classification from a regressor: logistic regression

Thresholding a linear regressor at 0.5 is not calibrated — nothing constrains the output to [0,1], and squared error over-penalizes confident correct predictions. Logistic regression instead squashes the linear score through a sigmoid and fits by maximum likelihood:

P(y=1 | x) = σ(w·x + b) = 1 / (1 + e−(w·x+b))Decision boundary P = 0.5 ⟺ w·x + b = 0 — still linear, just in the log-odds
J(w) = −Σᵢ [yᵢ log ŷᵢ + (1−yᵢ) log(1−ŷᵢ)]Cross-entropy / log loss — the MLE objective, convex, no closed form; solved by gradient descent

The knob that matters is complexity, not the algorithm name

  • Polynomial features make a linear model nonlinear in x while staying linear in the weights — same fit machinery, richer hypothesis space, the same bias–variance trade-off as the learning-curve topic: degree too low underfits, too high memorizes and swings wildly between points.
  • Regularization is the other dial. Ridge (L2) shrinks every weight toward 0 and handles correlated features gracefully; LASSO (L1) drives some weights to exactly 0, performing feature selection as a side effect.
Jridge(w) = Σᵢ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² + λ‖w‖₂²λ trades training fit for smaller weights — λ = 0 is plain least squares, λ → ∞ forces w → 0

Where points get lost

Scoring a probabilistic classifier with accuracy or squared error hides miscalibration — confidently wrong looks the same as honestly uncertain. Use log loss or AUC for logistic regression, not a regression metric or a bare hit rate, and say why in the report.

The logistic regression loss is convex — so why is there no closed-form solution?

Cross-entropy composed with a sigmoid is convex in w — a single global minimum — but setting its gradient to zero gives a transcendental equation (the sigmoid appears inside the sum with no algebraic inverse), not a linear system like squared error's normal equations. Convexity guarantees gradient descent finds the optimum; it does not hand you a formula for it.

Instance-based learning: k-NN

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine you move to a new neighborhood and want to guess if you'll like living there — instead of studying any rules, you just go ask your 5 nearest neighbors what they think, and go with whatever most of them say. k-NN never builds a rulebook at all; it just remembers every neighbor's opinion and, whenever you ask a new question, looks up whoever is closest and copies their answer.

Lazy learning: keep the data, defer all work to query time. There is no model — the training set is the model.

Lazy (k-NN)Eager (tree, net, SVM)
Training costO(1) — storeExpensive
Query costO(nd) per query (brute force)Cheap, ~O(depth) or O(#SV)
MemoryAll n pointsParameters only
Adapting to new dataFreeRetrain

The knobs, and what each one is really choosing

  • k — the bias/variance dial. k = 1: zero training error, jagged boundary, high variance. Large k: smooth, high bias, and at k = n you predict the global majority. Odd k avoids ties in binary problems.
  • Distance metric — this is the domain knowledge. Euclidean assumes every feature matters equally and is on the same scale, so you must standardize features; Manhattan is less sensitive to a single wild coordinate; a weighted or learned metric encodes what you know.
  • Weighting — distance-weighted voting (wi = 1/di²) lets you take large k without letting far points dominate; it is the discrete cousin of locally weighted regression.

Curse of dimensionality

To keep a fixed density of samples, the number of points needed grows exponentially in the number of features: covering [0,1]d at resolution 1/10 takes 10d cells. Worse, in high d the ratio of nearest to farthest distance approaches 1 — every point is roughly equidistant, so "nearest neighbour" stops meaning anything. This is the direct bridge to Unit 3: feature selection and PCA/ICA exist largely to make locality meaningful again.

Exam sentence

"k-NN's preference bias is that nearby points have similar labels and that all features contribute equally to nearness. Both halves fail in high dimensions, which is why dimensionality reduction helps k-NN far more than it helps trees."

Neural networks & backpropagation

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

Picture a long line of people playing a whispering game. The message gets garbled by the end, and the last person shouts "that's wrong!" Backprop is that last person turning around and telling the person before them exactly how much they messed it up, and that person turns around and blames the person before them, all the way back down the line — and everyone nudges how they whisper, just a tiny bit, so next time the message comes out better.

A perceptron computes ŷ = step(w·x + b) — a hyperplane. Stacking them with a differentiable nonlinearity turns a linear separator into a universal function approximator, and gradient descent does the credit assignment.

Two learning rules that are constantly confused

Backprop in five lines

forward: a(l) = σ(z(l)), z(l) = W(l)a(l−1) + b(l) output δ: δ(L) = ∇aE ⊙ σ′(z(L)) backward: δ(l) = (W(l+1)ᵀ δ(l+1)) ⊙ σ′(z(l)) gradient: ∂E/∂W(l) = δ(l) a(l−1)ᵀ update: W ← W − η ∂E/∂W (chain rule, reused bottom-up)

With sigmoid σ(z) = 1/(1+e−z), σ′ = σ(1−σ) ≤ 0.25 — multiply that through many layers and the gradient vanishes, which is the historical reason for ReLU (σ′ ∈ {0,1}).

Properties

  • Restriction bias: almost none. One hidden layer with enough sigmoid units approximates any continuous function on a compact domain; two layers handle arbitrary functions. So representation is not your problem — search is.
  • Preference bias: small random initial weights + gradient descent ⇒ simpler, smoother functions found first. This is why early stopping regularizes: you stop while the function is still simple.
  • Non-convex error surface: local optima, plateaus. Mitigations are the Unit 2 toolkit — momentum, random restarts, simulated annealing, and (in assignment 2) replacing backprop entirely with RHC/SA/GA on the weight vector.
  • Overfitting control: fewer/smaller units, weight decay (L2 = a Gaussian prior on weights), dropout, early stopping on a validation split.
Where points get lost

Claiming backprop "finds the global optimum." It finds a local optimum of a non-convex surface, and its trajectory — not just its endpoint — is the preference bias. Also: standardize inputs, or the network spends its early epochs undoing feature scale.

Support vector machines & kernels

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine red and blue marbles scattered on a table, too mixed up to separate with one straight line. The kernel trick is like tossing all the marbles up into the air — from up there, a flat sheet of paper can slice cleanly between the red ones and blue ones, even though no line worked down on the table. The clever bit: the math gets you the "from up there" answer without ever actually throwing anything.

Of all separating hyperplanes, take the one furthest from both classes. Maximizing the margin is the preference bias, and it is what makes the solution depend on only a handful of points.

maximize margin 2/‖w‖  ⟺  min ½‖w‖²  s.t. yi(w·xi + b) ≥ 1Soft margin: min ½‖w‖² + C Σξi, with slack ξi ≥ 0 for points inside the margin or misclassified

The Lagrangian dual is where the magic lives:

maxα Σi αi − ½ Σi,j αiαj yiyj ⟨xi, xj⟩  s.t. αi ≥ 0, Σαiyi = 0The data appear only as inner products — replace ⟨·,·⟩ with K(·,·) and you have the kernel trick

Most αi are zero; the points with αi > 0 are the support vectors — they lie on or inside the margin and alone determine the boundary. Delete every other training point and you get the identical classifier.

A valid kernel must satisfy Mercer's condition: the Gram matrix Kij = K(xi, xj) must be symmetric positive semi-definite — that guarantees an implicit feature map φ with K(x,z) = ⟨φ(x), φ(z)⟩ exists, so you never compute φ.

C — the slack penalty

Small C: wide margin, more violations tolerated, higher bias. Large C: hard margin, fits training data tightly, higher variance. C and γ interact — tune them on a 2-D grid, and say so.

γ — RBF width

Small γ: broad kernels, near-linear boundary. Large γ: each support vector influences only its immediate neighbourhood and the SVM degenerates toward 1-NN.

Where points get lost

Two traps. (1) Not scaling features — RBF distances are meaningless with mixed units. (2) Claiming the kernel "maps data to higher dimensions and then computes there." It never computes there; the whole point is that only inner products are needed.

Why is max-margin a sensible preference bias at all?

Two arguments the course accepts. Geometrically, the margin is the distance to the closest data, so it is the separator most tolerant to perturbation of any single point. Theoretically, generalization bounds for SVMs depend on the margin rather than on the dimension of the feature space — which is how an infinite-dimensional RBF space can still generalize. The same margin story reappears in boosting.

Ensembles: bagging & boosting

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

One weather forecaster is often wrong. But if you ask 100 forecasters — each having seen slightly different data — and go with the majority vote, you usually do better than any single one (that's bagging). Or you could train forecasters one after another, where each new one is specifically told to focus hardest on the days the previous forecasters got wrong (that's boosting) — a team that keeps patching its own mistakes.

Combine many weak rules into one strong one. Bagging attacks variance; boosting attacks bias — and that difference is the most-asked ensemble question in this course.

AdaBoost, exactly

D1(i) = 1/n for t = 1..T: ht ← weak learner trained on Dt εt = Σi Dt(i)·[ht(xi) ≠ yi] αt = ½ ln((1 − εt)/εt) Dt+1(i) = Dt(i)·exp(−αt yi ht(xi)) / Zt H(x) = sign(Σt αt ht(x))

Read the two formulas: αt goes to 0 as εt → ½ (a coin flip gets no vote) and grows without bound as εt → 0. The reweighting multiplies by e−α on correct points and e+α on errors, so the next learner sees a distribution concentrated where the ensemble is currently wrong.

Why boosting often does not overfit

Training error hits zero and test error keeps falling — the classic curve. The explanation the course gives is margins: even after every point is classified correctly, additional rounds keep pushing points further from the boundary, increasing the confidence margin, and the generalization bound depends on the margin distribution, not on T. The exception, which you should always name in the same breath: with noisy or mislabelled data boosting does overfit, because it will spend unbounded weight trying to fit points that are simply wrong.

Assignment 1 tactic

Run boosted stumps against a single pruned tree on both datasets and plot test error vs number of weak learners. If one dataset has label noise, you get the textbook contrast — one curve keeps improving, the other turns up — and that contrast, explained via margins and noise, is a full analysis paragraph.

Why do you boost stumps rather than fully-grown trees?

Boosting's guarantee assumes a weak learner — one that beats chance on any weighting. A fully grown tree already has near-zero training error on the first round, so ε₁ ≈ 0, α₁ explodes, the distribution barely changes and there is nothing left for later rounds to correct; you get one high-variance tree with extra steps. Weak learners leave residual error for the sequence to work on, and boosting reduces bias by summing many of them.

Computational learning theory: PAC & VC

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

You hire a fortune teller and want a guarantee: "if I show you enough example fortunes, you'll be right about the future at least 95% of the time." PAC learning is the math for figuring out exactly how many examples that guarantee needs. VC dimension measures how wildly flexible the fortune teller's stories can be — a teller who can spin any story to fit any facts (high VC dimension) needs way more examples before you should trust them, because they could just be making things up that happen to match what you showed them.

The unit that asks not "does it work?" but "how many examples before it must work?" Learn the three inputs, the two bounds, and what VC dimension means operationally.

The setting: a concept class C, hypothesis space H, unknown target c ∈ C, examples drawn i.i.d. from a fixed but unknown D. True error errorD(h) = Prx∼D[h(x) ≠ c(x)].

PAC — probably approximately correct

A learner PAC-learns C if for any ε, δ it outputs, with probability at least 1 − δ, a hypothesis with error at most ε, in time and samples polynomial in 1/ε, 1/δ and problem size. Approximately = the ε; probably = the δ.

Finite H, consistent learner (Haussler):  m ≥ (1/ε)(ln|H| + ln(1/δ))
Agnostic (best h may still err):  m ≥ (1/2ε²)(ln|H| + ln(1/δ))
Infinite H:  m ≥ (1/ε)(8·VC(H)·log₂(13/ε) + 4·log₂(2/δ))Note the ε → ε² penalty when you drop the realizability assumption

VC dimension

The largest number of points that H can shatter — i.e. for which some arrangement of that many points can be labelled in all 2d ways by members of H. To prove VC(H) = d you must do two things: exhibit one set of d points that is shattered, and argue that no set of d+1 points can be.

A class is PAC-learnable iff its VC dimension is finite. Sanity check the direction of every term: more accuracy (small ε), more confidence (small δ) and richer H (large VC) all increase the sample requirement.

Where points get lost

"Shatters d points" means there exists a set of size d that can be labelled every way — not every set. Three collinear points cannot be shattered by a line, but VC of a linear separator in the plane is still 3, because a triangle can.

What are mistake bounds, and how do they differ from PAC?

Mistake-bound analysis is online: examples arrive one at a time, the learner predicts, then sees the truth, and you count total mistakes over the whole (possibly adversarial) sequence — no distributional assumption at all. PAC is batch and distributional. The canonical results: the halving algorithm makes at most log₂|H| mistakes, and weighted majority makes O(log n + m) where m is the best expert's mistake count.

Bayesian learning & Naive Bayes

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

You have a hunch it might rain today (your prior). You look outside and see dark clouds (new evidence). Bayes' rule is just the honest, careful way to mix your old hunch with the new evidence into an updated, better hunch (the posterior). "Naive" just means: to keep the math easy, pretend every clue — clouds, wind, humidity — is totally unrelated to the others, which isn't quite true but works surprisingly well anyway.

Reframe learning as inference over hypotheses: instead of searching for a good h, ask what the data does to your belief about all of them.

P(h | D) = P(D | h) · P(h) / P(D)posterior ∝ likelihood × prior
  • MAP: hMAP = argmaxh P(D|h)P(h).
  • ML: hML = argmaxh P(D|h) — MAP with a uniform prior.
  • Bayes optimal classifier: don't pick a hypothesis at all; vote them all, weighted by posterior — argmaxv Σh P(v|h)P(h|D). No other classifier using the same H and prior has lower expected error, and it can output a label no single hypothesis in H predicts. It is also usually intractable, which is the whole motivation for approximations like Naive Bayes and for ensembles.

Two results that connect Bayes to the rest of the unit

Least squares is maximum likelihood

If y = f(x) + ε with ε ∼ N(0, σ²) i.i.d., then maximizing P(D|h) is exactly minimizing Σ(yi − h(xi))². Squared-error regression is not an arbitrary choice — it is the Gaussian-noise assumption written down. Similarly, an L2 weight penalty is a zero-mean Gaussian prior on the weights, so ridge/weight decay is MAP estimation.

MDL — minimum description length

Taking −log₂ of the MAP objective turns it into argminh [ length(h) + length(D | h) ]: bits to describe the hypothesis plus bits to describe the data given it. Occam's razor falls out of Bayes rule, and this is the principled version of "prefer short trees."

Naive Bayes

vNB = argmaxv ∈ V P(v) · Πi P(ai | v)the "naive" step: attributes conditionally independent given the class

The independence assumption is almost always false, yet the classifier is often excellent — because you only need the argmax to be right, not the probabilities. Its estimated probabilities are typically badly calibrated (pushed toward 0 and 1) even when its decisions are good; say that rather than reporting its confidences as if they were meaningful.

  • Cost: linear in data, one pass, O(nd); parameters grow as O(d·|V|) rather than exponentially — that is the entire payoff of the assumption.
  • Zero-frequency problem: one unseen attribute–class pair sends the whole product to zero. Fix with m-estimate / Laplace smoothing: (nc + mp)/(n + m).
  • Handles missing attributes gracefully (drop the factor), and is a strong text-classification baseline.

Randomized optimization

Find argmaxx ∈ X f(x) when you have no derivative, no convexity, and only the ability to evaluate f. Four algorithms, and a story about what each one remembers.

The framing: what each algorithm keeps

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

Four searchers are all looking for the tallest mountain peak on a foggy map, using only their feet — no map, no satellite view. They differ only in what each one remembers as they search: one remembers just where they're standing right now, one also remembers a bit of randomness to escape dead ends, one keeps a whole population of searchers who can "have babies" together, and one keeps a running theory about what mountain-shapes tend to look like.

The cleanest way to order this unit is by memory. Every step up the ladder retains more information about the search so far, and pays for it in evaluations per iteration.

Hill climbing & random restarts

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

You're blindfolded on a hilly field, trying to find the highest point, and the only thing you can do is feel which direction goes up and take a step that way. You'll definitely reach a peak — but maybe not the highest peak, just whichever hill you happened to start closest to. Random restarts just means: teleport somewhere else at random and climb again, several times, then keep whichever peak was tallest.

RHC(f, N): x ← random point loop: x′ ← argmaxn ∈ N(x) f(n) # or a random neighbour, for stochastic HC if f(x′) ≤ f(x): return x # local optimum x ← x′ Random-restart HC: run RHC k times from fresh random starts, keep the best.

Pure hill climbing is greedy and gets stuck at the first local optimum, on plateaus (no improving neighbour but not a peak) and on ridges (progress requires moving diagonally, which the neighbourhood may not allow). Restarts convert the algorithm from "find the basin I landed in" to "sample basins": if a fraction p of random starts land in the global basin, the expected number of restarts to find it is 1/p, and the chance of missing it after k restarts is (1−p)k. That is why RHC with enough restarts is a genuinely strong baseline and why beating it is the bar in assignment 2.

Where points get lost

Reporting RHC results without stating the restart count and the total evaluation budget. RHC with 200 restarts and SA with 200× more iterations are not comparable to "SA with the default schedule" — fix the evaluation budget across algorithms and say you did.

Simulated annealing

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

You're a marble rolling around a bumpy landscape hunting for the lowest point, but you can only feel the slope right under you — you can't see the whole map. Early on you're bouncing around like crazy (hot), even rolling uphill sometimes on purpose just to explore and escape shallow dips. As time passes you "cool down" and bounce less and less, until you're just gently settling into whichever valley you're in.

Hill climbing that is allowed to go downhill, with a probability that decays over time. The metaphor is metallurgical: heat the metal so atoms rearrange freely, then cool slowly enough that they settle into a low-energy crystal instead of freezing into a defect.

SA(f, N, T₀, cooling α): x ← random point; T ← T₀ repeat until T < Tmin: x′ ← random neighbour in N(x) ΔE ← f(x′) − f(x) if ΔE > 0: x ← x′ # always accept improvement else: x ← x′ with prob. eΔE/T # sometimes accept a worse point T ← α·T # geometric cooling, α ∈ (0,1)
P(accept a worse move) = exp(ΔE / T)T → ∞: accept anything — a random walk.   T → 0: accept nothing worse — pure hill climbing.

The two results to quote

  • With a sufficiently slow cooling schedule (logarithmic, Tt ∝ 1/log t), SA converges to the global optimum with probability 1. This is a theoretical guarantee, not a practical schedule — geometric cooling with α ≈ 0.95–0.999 is what you actually run, and you should note the gap.
  • At a fixed temperature the stationary distribution is Boltzmann: π(x) ∝ ef(x)/T. High T flattens it toward uniform; low T concentrates it on the optima. This is the single most quotable fact about SA and it also explains softmax exploration in Unit 4.
Tuning, in report language

T₀ should be high enough that early acceptance of worse moves is common (a rule of thumb: set T₀ so ~80% of uphill-and-downhill moves are accepted initially, which you can estimate from the spread of ΔE in a random walk). α controls the exploration/exploitation trade: cool too fast and SA degenerates into hill climbing from one random start; cool too slowly and you burn the whole budget on a random walk.

Genetic algorithms

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

Like breeding the fastest racehorses: keep the fastest ones from this generation, pair them up to make "foals" that mix traits from both parents (crossover), occasionally give a foal a random surprise trait (mutation), and repeat for many generations. Over time the whole population tends to get faster, because the winners keep passing on whatever made them win.

Keep a population instead of a point, and generate new candidates by recombining good ones. Crossover is the only genuinely new idea in the unit — it is a jump through the space that no local search can make.

Building blocks and deception

The intuition is the building-block hypothesis: short, low-order, high-fitness schemata (partial patterns like 1 1 * * * 0) are combined by crossover into better full solutions. That works when the problem is decomposable. A deceptive problem is one where the low-order building blocks point away from the global optimum — and there GA does poorly while something that models dependencies (MIMIC) or that can traverse the whole path (SA with enough patience) does better.

Where points get lost

Attributing GA success to mutation. Mutation alone plus selection is essentially parallel hill climbing. The claim to test — and to report on — is whether crossover is doing the work: run the same GA with crossover disabled and compare. That single ablation is worth more than another hyper-parameter sweep.

MIMIC

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

Most search methods are one person hunting for treasure alone — try a spot, adjust, try again. MIMIC instead keeps a big crowd of guessers, throws out the worst half, and asks "what do all my best guessers have in common?" It writes that pattern down as a simple recipe, then generates a whole new crowd who follow it — like a search party sharing notes on what's working, instead of everyone wandering off alone.

Mutual-Information-Maximizing Input Clustering. Instead of carrying points, carry a probability distribution over good points, and refine it. It is the only algorithm in the unit that conveys structure between iterations.

MIMIC(f, sample size N, keep quantile q): θ₀ ← uniform over X repeat: sample N points from Pθt(x) # currently: P(x | f(x) ≥ θ_t) θt+1 ← the q-th percentile fitness of that sample # raise the bar keep the points with f(x) ≥ θt+1 re-estimate the distribution from those survivors until θ stops rising

The estimated distribution is a dependency tree: MIMIC approximates the true joint by a chain/tree of pairwise conditionals and picks the tree that minimizes KL divergence to the truth, which is equivalent to a maximum-spanning-tree over pairwise mutual information (the Chow–Liu construction). That is why the algorithm's name mentions mutual information, and it is the link back to Unit 0.

Why it wins
  • Orders of magnitude fewer fitness evaluations — decisive when f is a simulation or a physical experiment.
  • It represents dependencies between variables, so it handles structured and deceptive problems the GA's blind crossover cannot.
  • The learned tree is itself interpretable output — you can read which variables interact.
Why it often loses in practice
  • Each iteration costs far more compute (sampling + estimating a tree over d² mutual informations).
  • Needs a large enough sample per iteration or the structure estimate is noise.
  • Wall-clock comparisons flatter the cheap algorithms; evaluation-count comparisons flatter MIMIC. Report both.

k-means (Lloyd's algorithm)

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

You're handed a scatter of dots and told "organize these into 3 friend groups." First you guess 3 random meeting spots. Everyone walks to whichever meeting spot is closest to them. Then each meeting spot moves to stand in the middle of its own group. Repeat — walk to the closest spot, spot moves to the new middle — until nobody needs to switch groups anymore.

k-means(X, k): initialize k centers # random points, or k-means++ repeat: assign: each x → nearest center (Voronoi partition) update: each center → mean of its assigned points until assignments stop changing

It is coordinate descent on one objective — within-cluster sum of squares:

J = Σj=1..k Σx ∈ Cj ‖x − μj‖²The assign step minimizes J over assignments with centers fixed; the update step minimizes J over centers with assignments fixed

Why it converges — the proof you should be able to sketch

Each step weakly decreases J (the mean is the unique minimizer of squared distance to a set of points, and reassigning to the nearest center cannot increase it), J ≥ 0 is bounded below, and there are finitely many possible partitions (kn), so it cannot cycle and must terminate — in finite time, at a local optimum. It emphatically does not find the global optimum; the global k-means problem is NP-hard.

The properties and failure modes

  • Assumes spherical, similar-sized, similar-density clusters, because the only geometry is Euclidean distance to a mean. It fails on elongated, nested or crescent-shaped clusters.
  • Sensitive to initialization — run it many times with different seeds and keep the lowest J; or use k-means++, which seeds centers with probability proportional to squared distance from the nearest existing center and comes with an O(log k) approximation guarantee in expectation.
  • Sensitive to scale and to outliers (a mean is not robust; k-medoids is).
  • Complexity O(n·k·d·i) per run.
  • Choosing k: elbow on J, silhouette score (mean of (b−a)/max(a,b) — cohesion vs separation, range −1..1), gap statistic, or — in this course's assignment — downstream supervised performance and label agreement measures like adjusted Rand index or normalized mutual information.

Soft clustering: EM and Gaussian mixtures

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

Two friends each baked cookies from a secret recipe, then mixed all the cookies together in one jar — now you can't tell which cookie came from which recipe. EM plays a guessing game: first guess which recipe each cookie probably came from, use those guesses to update what you think each recipe actually is, then re-guess the cookies using the better recipe idea, and repeat — round and round, getting more confident each time, even though nobody ever tells you the real answer.

k-means makes hard assignments. EM assigns responsibilities — each point belongs to every cluster with a probability — which lets clusters overlap and have shapes.

Hierarchical clustering & the impossibility theorem

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

You'd think there's one "correct" way to sort a big pile of LEGO bricks — by color? by size? by shape? Kleinberg proved something wild: you can write down 3 totally reasonable rules for what a "good sorting method" should do, and it's mathematically impossible for any method to follow all 3 at once. There's no such thing as one objectively "best" way to group things — it always depends on what you actually care about.

Single-linkage agglomerative clustering: start with n singleton clusters, repeatedly merge the two clusters whose closest pair of points is nearest, until k remain. Complexity O(n³) naively, O(n² log n) with a heap. The linkage choice changes everything:

Kleinberg's impossibility theorem

No clustering function on distances can satisfy all three of these simultaneously:

  • Richness — every possible partition of the points is achievable for some distance function.
  • Scale invariance — multiplying all distances by c > 0 leaves the clustering unchanged.
  • Consistency — shrinking within-cluster distances and stretching between-cluster distances leaves the clustering unchanged.

Each pair is achievable; all three are not. The practical reading, and the one to write down: clustering has no objectively correct answer. Every algorithm silently gives one of the three up (fixing k sacrifices richness; a fixed distance threshold sacrifices scale invariance), so a clustering result must be justified by what you do with it, not by the algorithm's authority.

Feature transformation: PCA, ICA, RCA, LDA

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

Imagine photographing a lumpy 3D sculpture, but you can only keep one flat photo. You'd pick the angle that shows the most interesting detail, not the boring flat side. PCA does exactly that to data: it finds the "best angle" to look from — the direction where the data is most spread out and interesting — and keeps that, throwing away the boring, flat angles. It doesn't know or care about labels or categories; it just chases variety.

Selection picks a subset of the original axes; transformation builds new axes as combinations of the old ones. Four methods, four different objectives — know which is which and you can answer almost any question in this section.

PCA, mechanically

1. center X (and usually standardize — otherwise the largest-unit feature wins) 2. C = XᵀX / (n−1) # covariance 3. eigendecompose C = VΛVᵀ # or take the SVD of X directly 4. keep the top m eigenvectors by eigenvalue 5. Z = X·Vm # projected data explained variance ratio of component i = λi / Σλ

Facts worth memorizing: eigenvalues are the variance along each component, they are non-negative, they sum to the total variance; the first PC is the direction of maximum variance and also the best-fitting line in a least-squares-perpendicular sense; components are mutually orthogonal; a zero eigenvalue means a linearly dependent direction. PCA is the optimal linear transformation for L2 reconstruction — which is exactly why it can destroy a discriminative signal that happens to live along a low-variance direction.

Where points get lost

Assuming high variance means high usefulness. PCA is unsupervised — it has never seen your labels. If the class-separating direction has small variance, PCA will discard it and downstream accuracy drops. Report that when it happens; it is a finding, not a failure.

ICA in one paragraph

ICA assumes observations are linear mixtures of statistically independent, non-Gaussian sources and recovers the unmixing matrix by maximizing non-Gaussianity (kurtosis or negentropy). It cannot recover Gaussian sources — a mixture of Gaussians is Gaussian and the rotation is unidentifiable — and it recovers sources only up to permutation and scale. On images, PCA components look like blurry global averages; ICA components look like edges. On a tabular dataset, ICA components with high kurtosis are the interesting ones.

Unit 4 · assignment 4 & the RL report

Reinforcement learning

Decisions over time, with delayed and scalar feedback. The MDP is the model; Bellman is the equation; everything else is how you solve it with or without knowing the model.

Markov decision processes

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

A video game level where the only thing that matters for what happens next is exactly where you're standing right now — not how you got there, not what you did five turns ago. That "only the present matters" rule is called the Markov property, and an MDP is just a formal way to describe a world with that rule: places you can be, moves you can make, and rewards you might get.

An MDP is the tuple ⟨S, A, T, R, γ⟩: states, actions, transition model T(s,a,s′) = P(s′ | s,a), reward R(s) (or R(s,a) / R(s,a,s′)), and discount γ ∈ [0,1).

The Markov property: the next state depends only on the current state and action, not on history. "Only the present matters" — and if it does not hold, you fix it by enlarging the state, not by ignoring it. Also assume stationarity: T and R do not change over time.

Return:   Gt = Rt + γRt+1 + γ²Rt+2 + … = Σk≥0 γkRt+kWith |R| ≤ Rmax, the infinite sum is bounded by Rmax/(1−γ) — that is why γ < 1 makes infinite horizons well-defined

What γ means: γ → 0 is myopic (only immediate reward); γ → 1 is far-sighted and mathematically slower to converge. A useful reading is that 1/(1−γ) is the effective horizon — γ = 0.9 ≈ 10 steps, γ = 0.99 ≈ 100 steps. In a gridworld with a small negative step cost, lowering γ or making the step cost more negative pushes the optimal policy toward short, risky routes; a positive step reward can make the optimal policy avoid terminating at all.

Where points get lost

Confusing a policy π : S → A (what to do everywhere, not just along one path) with a plan (a fixed action sequence). Under stochastic transitions a plan is useless after the first surprise — that is precisely why RL learns policies.

Bellman equations, value iteration, policy iteration

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

"How good is it to be right here?" equals "how good is my very next step, plus a slightly-discounted amount of however good things are after that — forever." The Bellman equation is just that idea written in math: the value of now is built out of the value of next, like Russian nesting dolls, where each doll holds a smaller, slightly-shrunk copy of the next one inside it.

Why it converges: the contraction argument

The Bellman operator B is a γ-contraction in the max norm: ‖BV − BU‖∞ ≤ γ‖V − U‖∞. By the Banach fixed-point theorem it has a unique fixed point (V*) and repeated application converges to it from any start, with error shrinking by a factor γ per sweep. Being able to state this — contraction, unique fixed point, geometric convergence, independent of initialization — is the highest-value fact in Unit 4.

The convergence conditions — quote all three

  1. Every state–action pair is visited infinitely often (so exploration must never fully stop).
  2. The learning rate satisfies the Robbins–Monro conditions: Σαt = ∞ and Σαt² < ∞ — e.g. αt = 1/t. A constant α violates the second and gives you a bounded oscillation instead of convergence, which is often what you want in a non-stationary problem.
  3. Finite MDP with bounded rewards.

The TD family

TD(0) updates from one step: V(s) ← V(s) + α[r + γV(s′) − V(s)] — low variance, biased by the current estimate (bootstrapping). Monte Carlo waits for the full return: unbiased, high variance, needs episodes to terminate. TD(λ) interpolates with eligibility traces: λ = 0 is TD(0), λ = 1 is (every-visit) Monte Carlo, and intermediate λ ≈ 0.7–0.9 is usually best because it spreads credit backward over the whole recent trajectory instead of one step at a time.

Bootstrapping, in one sentence

"TD methods update a guess toward a guess." That is the source of both their efficiency (learn from incomplete episodes, online) and their bias.

Exploration vs exploitation

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

You just moved to a new town with 20 restaurants. Do you keep going back to the one good restaurant you already found (exploit), or do you try a new random one tonight in case it's even better (explore)? Every smart strategy is really just a different rule for how often to try something new versus stick with your current favorite.

You cannot learn about actions you never take, and you cannot collect reward while experimenting. Everything in this section is a way of pricing that trade.

The bandit case (one state) is where the theory is clean: regret is the cumulative gap to the best arm, and the best algorithms achieve O(log t) regret. RL adds the complication that exploring changes which states you even reach.

Reward shaping

Adding intermediate rewards speeds learning but can change the optimal policy — the classic failure is an agent that farms the shaping reward in a loop instead of finishing. Potential-based shaping is the safe form: F(s,s′) = γΦ(s′) − Φ(s) for any potential function Φ. It provably leaves the optimal policy unchanged, and naming it is worth a mark whenever shaping comes up.

Game theory: when the environment has its own agenda

🧸 Explain like I'm 5

Playing rock-paper-scissors against someone smart: if you always throw rock, they'll figure it out and always throw paper to beat you. The only way to not get outsmarted is to mix up your throws randomly and unpredictably — and it turns out there's a mathematically "best" way to mix, called an equilibrium, where neither player can do any better by secretly changing their plan.

Everything since MDPs assumed one agent against a stationary world. Game theory is what happens when the "environment" is one or more other agents optimizing their own reward — the course's other major thread from Littman's own research (minimax-Q, 1994).

Normal-form games

A game is players, an action set per player, and a payoff for every action profile. Two ideas decide most of what you're asked to reason about:

  • Dominant strategy — an action at least as good as any other, against every possible action of the opponents. Iterated elimination of dominated strategies can shrink a game before any equilibrium search.
  • Zero-sum vs general-sum — in zero-sum, one player's gain is exactly the other's loss (Σ payoffs = 0); general-sum allows both to win or both to lose, which is where cooperation and threats become possible.
Player 2: CooperatePlayer 2: Defect
Player 1: Cooperate−1, −1−3, 0
Player 1: Defect0, −3−2, −2

Prisoner's dilemma: Defect dominates for both players individually, so (Defect, Defect) is the unique Nash equilibrium — even though (Cooperate, Cooperate) is Pareto-superior for both. Nash equilibrium is a statement about no player having a unilateral incentive to deviate, not about the outcome being good.

Nash equilibrium, and the zero-sum special case

Definition

A strategy profile is a Nash equilibrium if no player can improve their payoff by unilaterally changing their own strategy, holding everyone else's fixed. Every finite game has at least one — possibly in mixed strategies (a probability distribution over actions, needed whenever no pure-strategy equilibrium exists, e.g. matching pennies / rock–paper–scissors).

In two-player zero-sum games, Nash equilibrium coincides with the minimax solution — von Neumann's minimax theorem guarantees maxπ₁ minπ₂ V = minπ₂ maxπ₁ V, so "play your best strategy assuming the worst-case opponent" is not just cautious, it is optimal. That value is computable by linear programming, and it is exactly what minimax-Q generalizes to sequential games.

V* = maxπ₁ mina₂ Σa₁ π₁(a₁)·R(a₁,a₂)The minimax value of a matrix game — the best guaranteed payoff against an adversarial opponent

Repeated games: how cooperation survives self-interest

Play the prisoner's dilemma once and defection wins. Play it an unknown or infinite number of times and the calculus changes: a strategy like grim trigger (cooperate until the opponent ever defects, then defect forever) or tit-for-tat makes future cooperation conditional on past behaviour, turning "defect now" into a threat against future payoff.

Where points get lost

The folk theorem says any individually rational, feasible payoff can be sustained as a Nash equilibrium of the infinitely repeated game if the discount factor is high enough — a low discount factor (impatient players, or a game likely to end soon) collapses back to the one-shot equilibrium, and backward induction unravels cooperation entirely in a game with a known, finite horizon. State the discount-factor condition, not just "repetition enables cooperation."

Stochastic games: multi-agent MDPs

A stochastic (Markov) game generalizes an MDP to multiple agents: state, joint actions, a transition function shared by everyone, and one reward function per agent. Zero-sum stochastic games have a well-defined value found by minimax-Q — Q-learning's update with the max over the learner's own action replaced by the minimax value of the one-shot game at each state:

Q(s,a₁,a₂) ← Q(s,a₁,a₂) + α[r + γ·minimaxπ₁ Q(s′,π₁,·) − Q(s,a₁,a₂)]Minimax-Q (Littman, 1994) — converges to the equilibrium value under the same conditions as single-agent Q-learning

General-sum stochastic games are harder: no single "the" equilibrium value in general, multiple equilibria can coexist, and an agent's optimal policy depends on beliefs about what the others will do — why multi-agent RL is still an open problem rather than a solved special case of Unit 4.

Self-test: the questions that actually get asked

You add 10,000 more training examples and CV error does not move. What do you conclude, and what do you do?

High bias, not high variance — variance error shrinks with data and bias does not. The fix is a richer hypothesis space or better features: deeper tree, more hidden units, a nonlinear kernel, feature engineering. Adding data or regularizing further is wasted effort.

Boosting has driven training error to zero at round 40, yet test error is still falling at round 300. Explain.

Further rounds keep increasing the margin — the confidence with which points are classified — even though the sign of every prediction is already right. Generalization bounds for voted classifiers depend on the margin distribution rather than on the number of rounds, so test error can keep improving. Caveat to state in the same answer: with label noise this reverses, because AdaBoost's exponential reweighting pours unbounded weight onto mislabelled points.

Why is SA with a very high fixed temperature useless, and with a very low one also useless?

The acceptance probability is eΔE/T. As T → ∞ that tends to 1 for every move, so the search is an unbiased random walk that never exploits. As T → 0 it tends to 0 for every worsening move, so SA reduces to hill climbing and gets trapped in the first local optimum. The value is entirely in the schedule between the two — and the stationary distribution π(x) ∝ ef(x)/T shows exactly how the concentration on optima tightens as T falls.

A GA and a random-restart hill climber both solve Four Peaks, but the GA needs far fewer evaluations. Why?

Four Peaks rewards combining a prefix of 1s with a suffix of 0s. Each half is a separate building block that local search can discover, but a hill climber holding one half cannot acquire the other without descending through a large fitness valley. Crossover splices two parents that each solved one half, reaching the global optimum in a single operation. The general claim: GA beats local search when the problem decomposes into recombinable, independently-discoverable blocks.

PCA reduced your data to 10 components capturing 95% of variance, and your classifier got worse. Is PCA broken?

No — it did what it optimizes. PCA is unsupervised and maximizes retained variance, which is not the same as retained discriminability. If the class-separating direction has small variance (a subtle but consistent offset), it lands in the discarded 5%. Remedies: LDA (supervised), keep more components, or use a selection method scored on the actual learner. Reporting this as a finding — with the scree plot and the accuracy drop side by side — is a stronger result than a small accuracy gain.

Why does Q-learning converge to π* even when the agent behaves randomly the whole time?

Because the update target uses maxa′ Q(s′,a′), not the action the behaviour policy will actually take — it is off-policy. The behaviour policy only has to guarantee that every state–action pair is visited infinitely often; the learned values are those of the greedy policy regardless. With the Robbins–Monro learning-rate conditions and a finite MDP, Q converges to Q* with probability 1. Contrast SARSA, whose target uses the action actually taken, so it converges to the value of the exploring policy.

State the VC dimension of linear separators in ℝ² and prove it.

It is 3. Lower bound: three points in general position (a triangle) can be labelled in all 8 ways by some line — exhibit the cases. Upper bound: no set of 4 points can be shattered. Either one point lies inside the triangle of the other three (label it opposite to the rest and no line works), or the four form a convex quadrilateral (label the two diagonal pairs oppositely — XOR — and no line separates them). Since VC = d + 1 for ℝd, this generalizes.

Your k-means silhouette score peaks at k = 2, but the data has 7 true classes. What went wrong?

Probably nothing algorithmic. Silhouette measures geometric cohesion and separation under a Euclidean metric — it finds the structure the metric can see, which need not match semantic labels. Likely culprits: unscaled features letting one dominate distance; non-spherical or unequal-density classes violating k-means' assumptions; several true classes overlapping in feature space. Diagnose by scoring cluster–label agreement (adjusted Rand, NMI) as well as silhouette, and by re-clustering after PCA/ICA. Kleinberg's theorem is the honest framing: there is no k that is correct independent of what you want the clustering for.

When would you prefer policy iteration over value iteration, and vice versa?

Policy iteration when |S| is small enough that exact policy evaluation (an O(|S|³) linear solve, or a cheap iterative one) is affordable — it converges in very few iterations because the policy space is finite and each step strictly improves. Value iteration when the state space is large: each sweep is only O(|S|²|A|), and you can stop early since the greedy policy typically becomes optimal well before the values converge. Modified policy iteration (a few evaluation sweeps rather than an exact solve) is the usual compromise, and mentioning it shows you understand they are two ends of one dial.

Give the one-line inductive bias for each of: decision trees, k-NN, neural nets, SVMs, Naive Bayes.

Trees: prefer short trees with informative attributes near the root, axis-parallel splits. k-NN: nearby points share labels, all features weigh equally in the metric. Neural nets: nearly unrestricted representation, but gradient descent from small weights prefers smooth, low-complexity functions. SVM: among separators, prefer the maximum-margin one; the kernel fixes the space it lives in. Naive Bayes: the MAP hypothesis under the assumption that attributes are conditionally independent given the class.

Paradigm 1 — Supervised learning (20 questions)

Covers Unit 0 (foundations) and Unit 1 in full: bias, evaluation, every SL algorithm, and CLT. Click a question, think through your answer, then reveal it.

You have two hypotheses that both perfectly fit every training example you've seen. Without more data, is there any way to say one is "more likely correct" than the other?

Only via inductive bias — the training data alone cannot distinguish two hypotheses consistent with it, which is exactly what "underdetermined by data" means. Any preference between them must come from a bias baked into the learner (Occam's razor, MDL, a restricted hypothesis space), not from the data itself.

A classifier gets 99% accuracy on a dataset that is 99% one class. Is this a good result?

Not necessarily — a trivial "always predict the majority class" classifier also gets 99% accuracy. Accuracy alone is uninformative under class imbalance; you need precision/recall/F1/AUC, or accuracy compared against the majority-class baseline, to know if the model learned anything.

Why does k-fold cross-validation give a better estimate of generalization error than one train/test split?

A single split's estimate has high variance — it depends on which points happened to land in the test set, especially for small datasets. Averaging over k splits (each point tested exactly once) reduces that variance and uses every point for both training and validation.

Two students both add polynomial features to a linear regression. One's test error goes up, the other's goes down. What single fact explains the difference?

Which side of the bias–variance crossover each landed on, relative to their amount of training data. Added complexity helps until the hypothesis space becomes rich enough to start fitting noise instead of signal.

Why is the widest-margin separating hyperplane considered "safer" than one that barely separates the classes?

A wide margin leaves more room before a small perturbation — noise, measurement error, one different sample — flips a point to the wrong side. A tight margin is one bad point from misclassifying, and the SVM's generalization bound depends on the margin size directly, not on how many separating hyperplanes exist.

Why can a linear model in an infinite-dimensional kernel feature space still generalize well, against the usual "more dimensions → more overfitting" intuition?

Because the capacity measure that matters for SVMs is the margin achieved, not the dimensionality of the feature space — the generalization bound is stated in terms of margin, which can stay large even as the ambient dimension grows. The curse-of-dimensionality intuition applies to distance-based methods like k-NN, not to margin-based ones.

Bagging resamples data; boosting reweights data based on previous learners' mistakes. Why is bagging parallelizable and boosting not?

Bagging's learners are statistically independent — each resample is drawn without knowledge of the others, so every learner can train simultaneously. Boosting's t-th learner is a function of the (t−1)-th learner's errors, so each round strictly depends on the last and the sequence can't be parallelized.

A decision tree grown to full depth gets 100% training accuracy. Does that tell you it will generalize well?

No — a fully-grown tree can create one leaf per training point in the worst case, so 100% training accuracy is achievable by pure memorization. You need a validation/CV estimate or a learning curve to say anything about unseen data.

Why does Naive Bayes often work well even though its independence assumption is almost never true?

It only needs to rank the correct class highest, not estimate the true joint probability correctly — even badly miscalibrated probabilities can produce the right argmax. Errors from the independence assumption bias all classes' scores in similar, partly-cancelling ways, so accuracy degrades far less than calibration does.

The Bayes optimal classifier is proven to minimize expected error. Why isn't it used directly instead of every other algorithm?

It requires summing a prediction over every hypothesis in H weighted by its posterior, which is intractable for any nontrivial H. Nearly every practical algorithm — MAP, Naive Bayes, ensembles — is a tractable approximation to this uncomputable ideal.

An algorithm has zero restriction bias. Does that mean it has no bias at all, and no risk of poor generalization?

No — zero restriction bias just means the hypothesis space can represent almost any function; it says nothing about preference bias, which determines which representable hypothesis the search actually finds first. Backprop's preference for smooth, small-weight solutions is what makes it generalize, despite having almost no restriction bias.

Why does information gain favor an attribute that's a unique ID for every example, and why is that a problem?

A unique-ID split creates one perfectly pure child per example, so weighted-average child entropy is 0 and gain is maximal — but the split generalizes to nothing, since a new example's ID has no matching branch. Gain rewards purity, not future usefulness.

A neural net's error surface is non-convex. What does "gradient descent converged" actually guarantee?

Only that it reached a point of (approximately) zero gradient — a local minimum, saddle point, or plateau, not necessarily the global minimum. That's why initialization and trajectory (small random weights, momentum, restarts) are part of the model's effective preference bias, not just implementation detail.

In what sense is L2-regularized regression mathematically the same as adding a Bayesian prior?

Minimizing squared error plus an L2 penalty on the weights is exactly MAP estimation under a zero-mean Gaussian prior on the weights, where λ corresponds to the prior's precision. Both encode "prefer small weights unless the data strongly says otherwise."

Why does feature standardization matter enormously for k-NN and RBF-SVM but far less for a decision tree?

k-NN and RBF-SVM both compute distances directly, so a larger-scale feature dominates the distance regardless of relevance. A tree only ever asks "above/below a threshold" one feature at a time, so a feature's absolute scale doesn't affect which side of a split a point lands on.

A model's training-complexity curve shows low bias and low variance, but its CV curve is still rising with more data. What's going on?

That's not a bias/variance problem — it usually means you simply haven't hit diminishing returns yet. Get more data, or check whether the "low variance" reading is itself unstable from too little data. The diagnosis has to come from reading both curve shapes, not assuming one story fits every plot.

"No free lunch" says no learner beats another averaged over every conceivable target function. How does inductive bias let real algorithms escape that?

Real problems aren't drawn from "every conceivable function" — they have structure (smoothness, sparsity, locality). Inductive bias is the bet that the target function has the kind of structure the bias assumes, which is why a well-matched bias genuinely outperforms on real problems even though no bias wins in the theorem's uniform average.

Two SVMs share the same C but differ in γ. One has almost every point as a support vector; the other has very few. Which is more likely overfitting?

The one with almost every point as a support vector — a large γ makes each point's influence highly local, so the boundary wiggles around individual points, the SVM analogue of a high-variance fit. Few support vectors means the boundary is set by a small set of genuinely hard cases, closer to intended max-margin behaviour.

"Boosting reduces bias, bagging reduces variance" needs the caveat "assuming a weak base learner" for boosting specifically. Why?

Boosting's bias-reduction depends on each new learner correcting the ensemble's residual error — which only works if learners are weak, so there's error left to correct across many rounds. A strong base learner already drives training error near zero on round one, leaving nothing left for later rounds to fix.

A finite H has 1,000 hypotheses; an infinite H′ has VC dimension 5. Which needs more examples for the same (ε, δ) guarantee?

Neither answer is automatic — the finite bound scales with ln|H| = ln(1000) ≈ 6.9, the infinite bound scales with VC(H′) = 5 inside a bound with a larger constant. The conceptual point: "richness" — whether measured by |H| or VC dimension — is what drives the sample requirement in both formulas, not finiteness itself.

Why does "shatters d points" mean there exists one set of d points labelled every way, not that every set of d points is shattered?

Three collinear points can't be shattered by a line (no line can separate the middle point from both outer ones when they alternate labels), yet the VC dimension of linear separators in the plane is still 3, because some arrangement — a triangle — can be labelled all 8 ways. The definition only requires one witnessing set, which is why proving VC = d needs both a shattered example and proof that no set of d+1 points can ever be shattered.

Extra practice — Randomized optimization (14 questions)

Not one of the exam's three graded paradigms, but heavily tested inside SL (weight optimization) and worth knowing cold regardless.

A hill climber gets stuck at a local optimum. Does adding more random restarts guarantee it eventually finds the global optimum?

Not with a fixed, finite number of restarts — they only sample a finite set of starting basins, and a landscape with many local optima or one narrow global-optimum basin can be missed with high probability forever. Restarts raise the odds of success; they give no convergence guarantee the way SA's cooling schedule theoretically does.

Why does SA's acceptance rule eΔE/T let it do something plain hill climbing structurally cannot?

Accepting some worsening moves — with a probability depending on how much worse (ΔE) and how "hot" the search still is (T) — lets SA cross small fitness valleys to escape a local optimum. Pure hill climbing only ever accepts improving moves, so it can never step downhill even temporarily.

Why is crossover described as a move "no local search algorithm can make"?

Crossover combines two parents that can be arbitrarily distant in the search space into one offspring carrying good pieces of both. A local search step only ever looks within one step of its current position, so it can never jump between two solutions with no single-step path connecting them.

MIMIC keeps a probability distribution instead of a point or population. What concrete advantage does that give it on a problem like Four Peaks?

A probability model can directly capture dependency structure between variables — e.g. "front positions tend to be 1 exactly when back positions tend to be 0" — which is precisely the structure Four Peaks rewards. That lets MIMIC generate new high-fitness candidates that already respect the discovered structure.

MIMIC needs far fewer iterations than hill climbing but is called "expensive per iteration." Why?

Each iteration re-estimates a probability model (fitting a dependency structure over the survivors) and draws a fresh batch of samples, costing far more than one hill-climbing neighbour check. The trade is more work per step for a step that encodes much more information — worth it specifically when raw fitness evaluations are the expensive resource.

"No free lunch" says every optimizer performs equally well averaged over all possible fitness functions. Why doesn't that make choosing between RHC, SA, GA, MIMIC pointless?

Real problems are never drawn uniformly from "every possible function" — they have exploitable structure (ruggedness, deceptiveness, decomposability, expensive evaluation). The choice is about matching the algorithm's assumptions to the structure your specific problem actually has, not about which is "better" in general.

Why does Four Peaks specifically showcase a GA's advantage, rather than every deceptive problem doing so?

Four Peaks' deceptiveness comes from needing two separately-discoverable building blocks combined together — exactly what crossover is built to solve. A deceptive problem whose difficulty comes from pure ruggedness with no recombinable blocks wouldn't show the same GA advantage.

What's a fitness "plateau," and why is it a different challenge from a local optimum?

A plateau is a region where every neighbour has equal, not lower, fitness — a hill climber has no uphill signal to follow at all, unlike a local optimum where every neighbour is clearly worse. Algorithms need a way to keep moving on flat ground that pure greedy ascent doesn't provide.

Why can comparing RO algorithms by wall-clock time alone, without fitness evaluations, be misleading?

An algorithm can look "faster" purely because its per-iteration bookkeeping is cheaper, even if it needed far more actual fitness evaluations — and if the real-world fitness function is expensive, evaluations, not clock time, are the resource that matters. Reporting both separates sample-efficiency from raw computational overhead.

A GA's mutation rate is set to nearly zero. What failure mode does this risk, beyond ordinary premature convergence?

With almost no mutation, genetic diversity can only shrink — crossover recombines existing alleles but introduces none new — so the search becomes local search restricted to whatever alleles the initial population happened to contain. Any winning trait absent from generation zero becomes permanently unreachable.

Why might SA be used to train neural-net weights instead of gradient descent, despite the surface being differentiable?

Gradient descent only follows local slope, making it purely local-search despite the smooth surface. SA can accept worsening moves early on and explore more broadly before committing, at the cost of ignoring gradient information entirely when it's actually available — a genuine efficiency-vs-robustness trade-off.

Why does a cooling schedule that's too fast make SA behave like plain hill climbing?

If T drops to near-zero within a few iterations, eΔE/T for any negative ΔE also collapses to near-zero almost immediately, so SA stops accepting worsening moves almost from the start — exactly hill climbing's behaviour. The annealing advantage only exists in the window where T is neither too hot (random walk) nor too cold (hill climbing).

What does the "building block hypothesis" predict about which landscapes a GA does well on?

It says a GA's power comes from discovering, preserving, and recombining short, low-order, high-fitness substructures via selection and crossover. It predicts a GA excels on problems whose fitness decomposes into recombinable partial structures, and offers little advantage where no such decomposable structure exists.

Why might a larger GA population avoid premature convergence, and what's the practical cost?

More individuals maintain genetic diversity longer before selection narrows the population to near-identical individuals, giving crossover more distinct building blocks to combine. The cost is direct: fitness evaluations scale linearly with population size per generation, multiplying the cost of the most expensive step in the algorithm.

Paradigm 2 — Unsupervised learning (18 questions)

Clustering, feature selection, and feature transformation — the "no labels" half of the course.

k-means always converges, yet different random initializations produce different final clusterings. Why doesn't guaranteed convergence mean a guaranteed answer?

k-means' objective is non-convex in the cluster assignments, so it only guarantees convergence to a local minimum — different starting centers can lead to different local minima. That's why standard practice is multiple random restarts, keeping whichever run achieved the lowest final objective.

You run k-means with k=3 on data with 2 well-separated true clusters. What will it do, since it can't know the "true" number of groups?

It will still produce exactly 3 clusters, typically splitting one true cluster into two — k-means has no mechanism to merge or discard a center. Choosing k is a model-selection problem external to the algorithm (elbow method, silhouette score), not something k-means solves on its own.

EM's log-likelihood never decreases between iterations. Does that guarantee convergence to the global maximum-likelihood solution?

No — the monotonic guarantee is only about not going backward, not about reaching the global optimum. Like k-means, EM can converge to a local maximum depending on initialization, which is why it's also typically run from multiple starts.

Why is k-means the "limiting case" of EM on a Gaussian mixture, and what does that tell you about when to prefer each?

As the mixture's covariances shrink toward zero (spherical, equal, vanishing), EM's soft responsibilities collapse to effectively 0 or 1 for the nearest cluster, becoming k-means' hard assignment. Prefer EM/GMM whenever clusters plausibly overlap, differ in size, or aren't spherical — cases where hard assignment throws away real information.

Kleinberg's impossibility theorem shows no clustering function satisfies richness, scale-invariance, and consistency together. Does this mean clustering algorithms are broken?

No — it means an objectively "correct" universal clustering function is mathematically unachievable, not that any algorithm has a bug. Every real algorithm implicitly sacrifices one of the three properties (fixing k sacrifices richness), and that trade-off should be a deliberate, stated choice.

Why does single-linkage clustering suffer from "chaining"?

Single-linkage merges clusters based on their single closest pair of points, so a thin bridge of noisy points between two dense regions gets merged one link at a time into one long, stringy cluster — regions a human would consider clearly separate end up connected.

A feature is weakly but not strongly relevant. Can removing it ever hurt performance, even though the Bayes-optimal classifier doesn't strictly need it alone?

Yes — weak relevance means it only helps in combination with some other features. A learner that can actually exploit that interaction loses real accuracy if the feature is dropped, even though no single feature's absence breaks the theoretically optimal classifier.

Exhaustive feature subset selection is intractable. What does that force wrapper methods to do instead?

There are 2d subsets of d features, so evaluating every one with the actual learner is exponential. Wrappers substitute a greedy or randomized search (forward selection, backward elimination, or an RO technique) for the exhaustive search, trading a global-optimum guarantee for tractability.

What does a wrapper's CV-scored feature selection buy you that a filter (like ranking by information gain) cannot?

The wrapper's score directly reflects how well a specific learner performs with that subset, automatically accounting for feature interactions and that learner's own inductive bias — a filter scores features independently of any learner and structurally can't capture that. The cost is a far more expensive search.

PCA is applied before classification and accuracy gets worse. Does that mean PCA was implemented wrong?

Not necessarily — PCA's objective is entirely unsupervised and has no knowledge of class labels, so a high-variance direction can be irrelevant to classification while a low-variance direction is exactly what separates the classes. This is precisely when a supervised alternative like LDA is more appropriate.

ICA finds independent components, not merely uncorrelated ones like PCA. Why does that distinction matter for separating overlapping audio signals?

Independence is far stronger than zero correlation — uncorrelated variables can still be nonlinearly dependent — and true statistical independence, not just decorrelation, is what actually characterizes separate physical sources. PCA's variance-maximizing directions generally won't recover the original sources, because independent signals aren't in general the directions of maximum variance in their mixture.

Why does ICA provably fail on a mixture of Gaussian sources?

Gaussian distributions are uniquely closed under linear combination in a way that erases higher-order structure — any orthogonal rotation of jointly Gaussian independent variables produces another valid set of jointly Gaussian, uncorrelated variables, so there's no unique correct un-mixing direction to find. ICA fundamentally relies on non-Gaussianity to identify a unique separation.

Why can random projection (RCA) work well despite using zero information about the data?

The Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma guarantees that projecting into a sufficiently high-dimensional random subspace approximately preserves pairwise distances with high probability, regardless of the data's structure. Any distance-based downstream algorithm (k-NN, k-means) keeps roughly the same relative geometry — at the cost of preserving nothing meaningful like variance or class separability.

LDA finds at most (classes − 1) discriminant directions. Why exactly that limit?

LDA's directions come from the between-class scatter matrix, built from mean-difference outer products across classes, which has rank at most (classes − 1) — for C classes there are only C−1 linearly independent "mean difference" directions to begin with. Binary classification therefore yields exactly 1 discriminant direction, regardless of the original feature count.

1,000 features, 50 examples. Why is this worse for k-NN than for a heavily-regularized linear model?

k-NN's "nearby points are similar" assumption breaks down in very high dimensions relative to sample size — every point ends up roughly equidistant from every other, so nearest-neighbour carries almost no signal. A heavily regularized linear model restricts itself to few effective degrees of freedom regardless of raw dimensionality, so it can still generalize with few examples if the true relationship is close to linear.

A 2D PCA plot shows well-separated clusters. Does that guarantee your clustering algorithm's actual metric will show the same separation?

No — PCA is chosen to maximize variance for the eye's benefit in that plot, but the clustering metric may be computed on the full-dimensional (or differently-reduced) space, and clusters separated on the top-2 variance components can still overlap heavily along discarded dimensions. Visual separation is suggestive, not a guarantee.

Selection and transformation both reduce dimensionality. What's the core distinction between them?

Selection keeps a subset of the original features unchanged, preserving their real-world meaning; transformation builds entirely new axes as combinations of all original features, capturing spread-out structure but sacrificing interpretability. Choosing between them is really choosing whether individual feature interpretability matters downstream.

Why is average-linkage clustering called a "compromise" between single- and complete-linkage?

Single-linkage (nearest pair) is prone to chaining through noise; complete-linkage (farthest pair) is prone to breaking elongated clusters apart and is oversensitive to outliers. Average-linkage's mean pairwise distance dampens both failure modes by requiring broad agreement across many point pairs rather than one extreme pair.

Paradigm 3 — Reinforcement learning (18 questions)

MDPs, Bellman, TD methods, exploration, and game theory.

Why must an MDP's transitions depend only on the current state and action, not the full history — and what do you do if your problem seems to violate this?

The Markov property is what makes value functions and the Bellman equations well-defined and solvable — without it, "state" doesn't fully summarize what matters for the future, and VI/PI/Q-learning break down. The fix is to enlarge the state definition until the property holds again, not to abandon the framework.

Why does the Bellman optimality equation need a max over actions, while the equation for a fixed policy π does not?

Vπ just evaluates one already-decided policy — nothing to choose, you sum expected reward under π. V* is defined as the value under the best possible behaviour at every state, so it must consider every action's consequence and take the highest; the max is literally what "optimal" means, encoded into the equation.

Why does γ < 1 matter for a well-defined infinite-horizon return, beyond just "preferring reward sooner"?

With rewards bounded and γ < 1, the infinite geometric series converges to a finite value (Rmax/(1−γ)), so every policy has a well-defined, comparable value. With γ = 1, two policies that both collect +1 reward forever (one starting a step later) both sum to infinity and become mathematically indistinguishable.

Value iteration's sweeps are cheaper than policy iteration's evaluation step, yet PI is often faster in practice. Why?

PI converges in a small, finite number of outer iterations — there are only finitely many policies and each iteration strictly improves, a bounded process — so the expensive evaluation happens only a handful of times. VI's cheap sweeps repeat far more times (asymptotic convergence), and the total work can end up larger despite the lower per-step cost.

Why does the Bellman operator being a γ-contraction guarantee value iteration converges to the same answer regardless of initialization?

A γ-contraction shrinks the distance between any two value functions by a factor of γ each application, and the Banach fixed-point theorem guarantees a unique fixed point that repeated application reaches from any start. That's why initializing V₀ to anything — even all zeros — still provably converges to the same V*.

Q-learning is "off-policy." What does that mean, and why does it let Q-learning learn π* even under purely random behaviour?

The values learned correspond to a different policy than the one generating behaviour — the update always targets the greedy action's value regardless of which action was actually taken. Since the target always aims at the greedy policy, the behaviour policy only needs to guarantee every state-action pair keeps getting visited infinitely often.

SARSA is "on-policy." In a gridworld with a cliff, why does SARSA typically learn a safer path than Q-learning?

SARSA's target uses the value of the action actually taken next, including occasional exploratory missteps, so the risk of exploring off the cliff gets baked into the learned values near the edge. Q-learning's target always assumes the greedy action comes next, learning the optimal path as if exploration mistakes never happen — accurate but riskier if exploration is still ongoing.

Why do the Robbins–Monro conditions on the learning rate matter for Q-learning, and what breaks with a constant α?

Σα = ∞ ensures early updates never get permanently frozen in; Σα² < ∞ ensures the update magnitude eventually shrinks enough to actually settle. A constant α satisfies the first but not the second — Q-values keep oscillating instead of converging, useful in non-stationary settings but incompatible with a convergence guarantee in a stationary one.

Why is TD(0) described as biased but lower-variance than Monte Carlo?

TD(0)'s target depends on the current value estimate V(s′), which is itself just a guess — that's the bias. But it only depends on one transition's randomness, whereas Monte Carlo's full return accumulates randomness from every reward until termination, making it unbiased but far noisier episode to episode.

Why does TD(λ) with intermediate λ often beat both TD(0) and Monte Carlo?

Eligibility traces spread credit backward across recently visited states in proportion to λ, rather than crediting only the previous state (λ=0) or waiting for the entire episode (λ=1). That interpolation captures more true signal per update than one-step bootstrapping while avoiding the full variance of a complete trajectory.

Why does optimistic initialization only drive exploration in stationary environments?

It makes every action look attractive until tried, but once every action has been sampled a few times the optimism is "used up" and the agent settles into pure exploitation — fine if true values never change. In a non-stationary environment, an action's value can degrade later with no mechanism to re-encourage exploring it, since optimism only front-loads exploration once, at the start.

Why is potential-based reward shaping provably safe while an arbitrary hand-crafted bonus is not?

Potential-based shaping telescopes along any trajectory to a value depending only on start and end states, not the path taken — so it can't change which policy is optimal, only how fast learning gets there. An arbitrary bonus has no such structure, so an agent can loop to farm it without completing the task, because it genuinely changed which policy is optimal.

In a POMDP, why can't the agent just act on its most recent observation the way it would act on true state in a fully-observable MDP?

A single observation is a noisy, partial signal, so acting on it alone discards everything inferred from the full history. The correct thing to act on is the belief state — a full probability distribution over states, updated by Bayes' rule from the whole history — which is why solving a POMDP means solving a continuous-state belief MDP.

Using the prisoner's dilemma, why isn't a Nash equilibrium the same as the "best" outcome for the players?

Nash equilibrium is purely about individual incentives — no player can improve by unilaterally deviating — and says nothing about whether the outcome is good collectively. (Defect, Defect) is the unique equilibrium, yet (Cooperate, Cooperate) is strictly better for both; stable is not the same as optimal.

Why does von Neumann's minimax theorem let you treat "play defensively against the worst case" as literally optimal in two-player zero-sum games?

The theorem proves max-min exactly equals min-max in two-player zero-sum games — the value you can guarantee against a worst-case opponent equals what the opponent can hold you to. Because those coincide exactly, the "defensive" minimax strategy IS the equilibrium strategy, a coincidence specific to the zero-sum case.

The folk theorem sustains cooperation in an infinitely repeated prisoner's dilemma. Why must the discount factor be high enough?

Sustaining cooperation relies on a credible future punishment threat outweighing today's one-time gain from defecting, and the discount factor measures exactly how much that future threat is worth now. Too low a discount (impatient players, or a likely-to-end game) shrinks the punishment's discounted value below the immediate gain, and the threat stops deterring defection.

Why is replacing the max with a minimax value the right generalization for Q-learning in a two-player zero-sum stochastic game?

In a single-agent MDP, the agent alone controls the action, so greedy max correctly represents "act optimally." In a zero-sum stochastic game, the outcome depends on both players, so the agent should only guarantee what it can secure against an adversarial best response — exactly the one-shot minimax value at each state.

Why is finding a general-sum stochastic game's equilibrium fundamentally harder than a zero-sum one's, beyond "the math is harder"?

Zero-sum games have a single well-defined minimax value both players agree on, computable by LP. General-sum games can have multiple distinct Nash equilibria with different payoffs to each player, with no guarantee players agree on which to coordinate toward — a genuine coordination problem, not just a harder computation.
