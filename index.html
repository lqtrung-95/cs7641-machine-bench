Unit 0 · cross-cutting

Foundations you are graded on everywhere

Every report in this course is really an argument about bias, variance and evidence. Get these five ideas exact and the other three units become bookkeeping.

What "learning" means here

Mitchell's definition is the one the course leans on: a program learns from experience E with respect to task T and performance measure P if its performance at T, measured by P, improves with E. Every design decision you make in an assignment is a choice of one of those three letters.

Isbell and Littman frame the whole course as generalization, not memorization: induction from examples to a function that behaves on data you have never seen. The three families map cleanly onto what supervision you are given:

FamilyGivenSoughtThe real question
SupervisedPairs (x, y)Function approximationWhich of the infinitely many consistent functions do I prefer?
UnsupervisedOnly xCompact descriptionWhat structure justifies calling two points "the same"?
ReinforcementStates, actions, delayed scalar rewardPolicyWhich action now, given that the payoff arrives much later?
Say it this way in a report

"Induction is under-determined; the learner's inductive bias is what breaks the tie." That single sentence is the through-line of Unit 1, and graders reward candidates who use it to explain why two algorithms disagree on the same data instead of merely reporting that they do.

Inductive bias: restriction vs preference

A hypothesis space H plus a search order is a bias. Split it in two — the distinction shows up in almost every exam question:

  • Restriction bias — what H can represent at all. Linear regression restricts you to hyperplanes; a depth-1 tree restricts you to axis-aligned single splits.
  • Preference bias — which hypothesis inside H the algorithm reaches first. ID3 prefers shorter trees with high-information-gain attributes near the root; backprop prefers small weights and smooth functions because it starts small and moves by gradient.
AlgorithmRestriction biasPreference bias
Decision tree (ID3)All discrete functions (unrestricted for discrete inputs); axis-parallel splitsShort trees, high info-gain attributes at top, correct over incorrect
k-NNNothing excluded — any function representable in the limitLocality: near points have similar labels; all features matter equally (in Euclidean distance); smoothness
Neural netVery little; boolean, continuous, arbitrary (given enough units/layers)Smooth interpolation, low-complexity/small-weight solutions first
Linear SVMHyperplanes (in the kernel-induced space)Maximum margin — the single most "confident" separator
Naive BayesProducts of per-attribute conditionalsMAP hypothesis under conditional independence
Where points get lost

Writing "k-NN has no bias because it is non-parametric." It has a very strong preference bias — the locality/smoothness assumption plus equal feature weighting — and that assumption is exactly what the curse of dimensionality destroys.

Two learners fit the training set perfectly but disagree on a new point. What explains the disagreement?

Only the inductive bias. The data alone cannot distinguish them — both are consistent hypotheses. The difference must come from their restriction bias (one cannot represent what the other does) or preference bias (both could represent both answers, but their search reaches different ones first).

Bias–variance decomposition, and how to read a learning curve

For squared loss with target y = f(x) + ε, Var(ε) = σ², the expected error of a learner trained on random training sets decomposes exactly:

E[(y − ĥ(x))²] = (E[ĥ(x)] − f(x))² + E[(ĥ(x) − E[ĥ(x)])²] + σ²bias²  +  variance  +  irreducible noise

Bias is error from a hypothesis space too small or too rigid to contain the truth — it does not shrink with more data. Variance is sensitivity to which training sample you happened to draw — it does shrink with more data. This is why the two diagnostic curves say different things:

Model-complexity curve (x = complexity)
  • Train error falls monotonically.
  • CV error is U-shaped.
  • Left of the minimum: underfit / high bias.
  • Right of the minimum: overfit / high variance.
Learning curve (x = training-set size)
  • Curves converge high and flat → high bias; more data will not help, change the model.
  • Wide persistent gap, CV still descending → high variance; more data (or regularization) will help.
Assignment 1 tactic

Every SL report wants both curve types per algorithm per dataset. Plot them with error bars over folds or seeds, and write the sentence the plot supports — "the gap does not close by 100% of the data, so this is variance, which is why pruning at depth 6 improves CV by 4 points." Plots without that sentence are what "insufficient analysis" means in the rubric.

Information theory in one page

Entropy is the expected number of bits to encode a draw from P; everything else in the unit is arithmetic on it.

H(X) = − Σx P(x) log₂ P(x)   ·   H(X|Y) = Σy P(y) H(X | Y=y)
I(X;Y) = H(X) − H(X|Y) = H(X) + H(Y) − H(X,Y)Mutual information is symmetric, non-negative, and zero exactly when X ⫫ Y

Two facts worth memorizing: entropy of a binary variable is maximal (1 bit) at p = 0.5 and 0 at p ∈ {0,1}; and KL divergence D(P‖Q) = Σ P(x) log(P(x)/Q(x)) is non-negative, zero iff P = Q, and not symmetric — so it is a divergence, not a distance. KL is the objective behind MIMIC's dependency-tree projection and behind maximum-likelihood fitting generally.

Where points get lost

Calling KL a metric. It fails symmetry and the triangle inequality. Also: information gain is mutual information between the attribute and the label — saying so out loud connects Unit 1 to Unit 3's feature selection and earns credit.

The test set is a one-shot instrument. Every choice you make after looking at it leaks. The defensible pipeline:

Unit 1 · assignment 1 & the SL report

Supervised learning

Function approximation from labelled examples. Five algorithm families, one theory chapter, and one Bayesian chapter that quietly explains all of them.

Decision trees & ID3

A tree is a sequence of questions; learning one is choosing the order of the questions. ID3 is greedy and top-down: pick the attribute with the highest information gain, split, recurse.

ID3(Examples, Target, Attributes) if all Examples have same label → return leaf(label) if Attributes empty → return leaf(majority label) A ← argmaxa ∈ Attributes Gain(Examples, a) root ← A for each value v of A: Sv ← {e ∈ Examples : e.A = v} if Sv empty → child = leaf(majority label of Examples) else child = ID3(Sv, Target, Attributes − {A}) return root
Gain(S, A) = H(S) − Σv ∈ values(A) (|Sv|/|S|) · H(Sv)= I(label ; A) — the entropy you expect to remove by asking A

The properties you must be able to state

  • Greedy, no backtracking — reaches a locally good tree, not the globally shortest one; finding the optimal tree is NP-hard.
  • Bias: restriction = axis-parallel splits (so an XOR-in-two-continuous-features boundary needs a staircase); preference = short trees, informative attributes at the top.
  • Gain is biased toward many-valued attributes — a unique ID column has maximal gain and zero generalization. Fix with gain ratio (divide by the split's own entropy, C4.5) or Gini impurity.
  • Continuous attributes get thresholded: sort values, consider midpoints where the label changes. An attribute can be reused down a path (unlike discrete ID3, where it is exhausted).
  • Overfitting control: pre-pruning (max depth, min samples per leaf, gain threshold) or post-pruning (grow full, then reduced-error pruning against a validation set, or rule post-pruning). Post-pruning is generally stronger because early stopping can miss a split that only pays off in combination.
Gini(S) = 1 − Σi pi²   vs   H(S) = − Σi pi log₂ piBoth peak at the uniform distribution; Gini is cheaper (no log) and nearly always picks the same split
Where points get lost

Saying deeper trees are "more accurate." Deeper trees have lower training error and higher variance. In a report, the depth-vs-CV-error curve is the evidence; the sentence is "past depth d the tree begins fitting sample-specific noise, and CV error rises while train error goes to zero."

Why can a decision tree represent any boolean function, and what does that cost?

With enough depth you can enumerate every row of the truth table as a root-to-leaf path — so the restriction bias for discrete inputs is essentially none. The cost is size: parity/XOR over n attributes needs O(2ⁿ) nodes because no single attribute has any information gain on its own, so the greedy heuristic is also blind at the root.

Instance-based learning: k-NN

Lazy learning: keep the data, defer all work to query time. There is no model — the training set is the model.

Lazy (k-NN)Eager (tree, net, SVM)
Training costO(1) — storeExpensive
Query costO(nd) per query (brute force)Cheap, ~O(depth) or O(#SV)
MemoryAll n pointsParameters only
Adapting to new dataFreeRetrain

The knobs, and what each one is really choosing

  • k — the bias/variance dial. k = 1: zero training error, jagged boundary, high variance. Large k: smooth, high bias, and at k = n you predict the global majority. Odd k avoids ties in binary problems.
  • Distance metric — this is the domain knowledge. Euclidean assumes every feature matters equally and is on the same scale, so you must standardize features; Manhattan is less sensitive to a single wild coordinate; a weighted or learned metric encodes what you know.
  • Weighting — distance-weighted voting (wi = 1/di²) lets you take large k without letting far points dominate; it is the discrete cousin of locally weighted regression.

Curse of dimensionality

To keep a fixed density of samples, the number of points needed grows exponentially in the number of features: covering [0,1]d at resolution 1/10 takes 10d cells. Worse, in high d the ratio of nearest to farthest distance approaches 1 — every point is roughly equidistant, so "nearest neighbour" stops meaning anything. This is the direct bridge to Unit 3: feature selection and PCA/ICA exist largely to make locality meaningful again.

Exam sentence

"k-NN's preference bias is that nearby points have similar labels and that all features contribute equally to nearness. Both halves fail in high dimensions, which is why dimensionality reduction helps k-NN far more than it helps trees."

k-NN decision boundary — drag k, watch variance

Neural networks & backpropagation

A perceptron computes ŷ = step(w·x + b) — a hyperplane. Stacking them with a differentiable nonlinearity turns a linear separator into a universal function approximator, and gradient descent does the credit assignment.

Two learning rules that are constantly confused

Perceptron ruleDelta / LMS rule
UpdateΔw = η(y − ŷ)xΔw = η(y − w·x)x
Output usedThresholded ŷUnthresholded activation
GuaranteeConverges in finite steps if data are linearly separableConverges asymptotically to the min-squared-error solution even if not separable
SurfaceNo gradient (step is not differentiable)Gradient descent on a convex quadratic

Backprop in five lines

forward: a(l) = σ(z(l)), z(l) = W(l)a(l−1) + b(l) output δ: δ(L) = ∇aE ⊙ σ′(z(L)) backward: δ(l) = (W(l+1)ᵀ δ(l+1)) ⊙ σ′(z(l)) gradient: ∂E/∂W(l) = δ(l) a(l−1)ᵀ update: W ← W − η ∂E/∂W (chain rule, reused bottom-up)

With sigmoid σ(z) = 1/(1+e−z), σ′ = σ(1−σ) ≤ 0.25 — multiply that through many layers and the gradient vanishes, which is the historical reason for ReLU (σ′ ∈ {0,1}).

Properties

  • Restriction bias: almost none. One hidden layer with enough sigmoid units approximates any continuous function on a compact domain; two layers handle arbitrary functions. So representation is not your problem — search is.
  • Preference bias: small random initial weights + gradient descent ⇒ simpler, smoother functions found first. This is why early stopping regularizes: you stop while the function is still simple.
  • Non-convex error surface: local optima, plateaus. Mitigations are the Unit 2 toolkit — momentum, random restarts, simulated annealing, and (in assignment 2) replacing backprop entirely with RHC/SA/GA on the weight vector.
  • Overfitting control: fewer/smaller units, weight decay (L2 = a Gaussian prior on weights), dropout, early stopping on a validation split.
Where points get lost

Claiming backprop "finds the global optimum." It finds a local optimum of a non-convex surface, and its trajectory — not just its endpoint — is the preference bias. Also: standardize inputs, or the network spends its early epochs undoing feature scale.

Support vector machines & kernels

Of all separating hyperplanes, take the one furthest from both classes. Maximizing the margin is the preference bias, and it is what makes the solution depend on only a handful of points.

maximize margin 2/‖w‖  ⟺  min ½‖w‖²  s.t. yi(w·xi + b) ≥ 1Soft margin: min ½‖w‖² + C Σξi, with slack ξi ≥ 0 for points inside the margin or misclassified

The Lagrangian dual is where the magic lives:

maxα Σi αi − ½ Σi,j αiαj yiyj ⟨xi, xj⟩  s.t. αi ≥ 0, Σαiyi = 0The data appear only as inner products — replace ⟨·,·⟩ with K(·,·) and you have the kernel trick

Most αi are zero; the points with αi > 0 are the support vectors — they lie on or inside the margin and alone determine the boundary. Delete every other training point and you get the identical classifier.

Kernels

KernelFormNotes
LinearxᵀzBaseline; fast; use when d ≫ n
Polynomial(xᵀz + c)pFeature space = all monomials up to degree p
RBF / Gaussianexp(−γ‖x − z‖²)Infinite-dimensional feature space; large γ ⇒ narrow, wiggly, high variance
Sigmoidtanh(κ xᵀz + θ)Not PSD for all parameters

A valid kernel must satisfy Mercer's condition: the Gram matrix Kij = K(xi, xj) must be symmetric positive semi-definite — that guarantees an implicit feature map φ with K(x,z) = ⟨φ(x), φ(z)⟩ exists, so you never compute φ.

C — the slack penalty

Small C: wide margin, more violations tolerated, higher bias. Large C: hard margin, fits training data tightly, higher variance. C and γ interact — tune them on a 2-D grid, and say so.

γ — RBF width

Small γ: broad kernels, near-linear boundary. Large γ: each support vector influences only its immediate neighbourhood and the SVM degenerates toward 1-NN.

Where points get lost

Two traps. (1) Not scaling features — RBF distances are meaningless with mixed units. (2) Claiming the kernel "maps data to higher dimensions and then computes there." It never computes there; the whole point is that only inner products are needed.

Why is max-margin a sensible preference bias at all?

Two arguments the course accepts. Geometrically, the margin is the distance to the closest data, so it is the separator most tolerant to perturbation of any single point. Theoretically, generalization bounds for SVMs depend on the margin rather than on the dimension of the feature space — which is how an infinite-dimensional RBF space can still generalize. The same margin story reappears in boosting.

Ensembles: bagging & boosting

Combine many weak rules into one strong one. Bagging attacks variance; boosting attacks bias — and that difference is the most-asked ensemble question in this course.

AdaBoost, exactly

D1(i) = 1/n for t = 1..T: ht ← weak learner trained on Dt εt = Σi Dt(i)·[ht(xi) ≠ yi] αt = ½ ln((1 − εt)/εt) Dt+1(i) = Dt(i)·exp(−αt yi ht(xi)) / Zt H(x) = sign(Σt αt ht(x))

Read the two formulas: αt goes to 0 as εt → ½ (a coin flip gets no vote) and grows without bound as εt → 0. The reweighting multiplies by e−α on correct points and e+α on errors, so the next learner sees a distribution concentrated where the ensemble is currently wrong.

Why boosting often does not overfit

Training error hits zero and test error keeps falling — the classic curve. The explanation the course gives is margins: even after every point is classified correctly, additional rounds keep pushing points further from the boundary, increasing the confidence margin, and the generalization bound depends on the margin distribution, not on T. The exception, which you should always name in the same breath: with noisy or mislabelled data boosting does overfit, because it will spend unbounded weight trying to fit points that are simply wrong.

Assignment 1 tactic

Run boosted stumps against a single pruned tree on both datasets and plot test error vs number of weak learners. If one dataset has label noise, you get the textbook contrast — one curve keeps improving, the other turns up — and that contrast, explained via margins and noise, is a full analysis paragraph.

Why do you boost stumps rather than fully-grown trees?

Boosting's guarantee assumes a weak learner — one that beats chance on any weighting. A fully grown tree already has near-zero training error on the first round, so ε₁ ≈ 0, α₁ explodes, the distribution barely changes and there is nothing left for later rounds to correct; you get one high-variance tree with extra steps. Weak learners leave residual error for the sequence to work on, and boosting reduces bias by summing many of them.

Computational learning theory: PAC & VC

The unit that asks not "does it work?" but "how many examples before it must work?" Learn the three inputs, the two bounds, and what VC dimension means operationally.

The setting: a concept class C, hypothesis space H, unknown target c ∈ C, examples drawn i.i.d. from a fixed but unknown D. True error errorD(h) = Prx∼D[h(x) ≠ c(x)].

PAC — probably approximately correct

A learner PAC-learns C if for any ε, δ it outputs, with probability at least 1 − δ, a hypothesis with error at most ε, in time and samples polynomial in 1/ε, 1/δ and problem size. Approximately = the ε; probably = the δ.

Finite H, consistent learner (Haussler):  m ≥ (1/ε)(ln|H| + ln(1/δ))
Agnostic (best h may still err):  m ≥ (1/2ε²)(ln|H| + ln(1/δ))
Infinite H:  m ≥ (1/ε)(8·VC(H)·log₂(13/ε) + 4·log₂(2/δ))Note the ε → ε² penalty when you drop the realizability assumption

VC dimension

The largest number of points that H can shatter — i.e. for which some arrangement of that many points can be labelled in all 2d ways by members of H. To prove VC(H) = d you must do two things: exhibit one set of d points that is shattered, and argue that no set of d+1 points can be.

Hypothesis classVC dimension
Thresholds on a line x ≥ a1
Intervals [a,b] on a line2
Linear separators in ℝdd + 1
Axis-aligned rectangles in the plane4
Convex polygons in the plane∞
Finite H≤ log₂|H|

A class is PAC-learnable iff its VC dimension is finite. Sanity check the direction of every term: more accuracy (small ε), more confidence (small δ) and richer H (large VC) all increase the sample requirement.

Where points get lost

"Shatters d points" means there exists a set of size d that can be labelled every way — not every set. Three collinear points cannot be shattered by a line, but VC of a linear separator in the plane is still 3, because a triangle can.

What are mistake bounds, and how do they differ from PAC?

Mistake-bound analysis is online: examples arrive one at a time, the learner predicts, then sees the truth, and you count total mistakes over the whole (possibly adversarial) sequence — no distributional assumption at all. PAC is batch and distributional. The canonical results: the halving algorithm makes at most log₂|H| mistakes, and weighted majority makes O(log n + m) where m is the best expert's mistake count.

Bayesian learning & Naive Bayes

Reframe learning as inference over hypotheses: instead of searching for a good h, ask what the data does to your belief about all of them.

P(h | D) = P(D | h) · P(h) / P(D)posterior ∝ likelihood × prior
  • MAP: hMAP = argmaxh P(D|h)P(h).
  • ML: hML = argmaxh P(D|h) — MAP with a uniform prior.
  • Bayes optimal classifier: don't pick a hypothesis at all; vote them all, weighted by posterior — argmaxv Σh P(v|h)P(h|D). No other classifier using the same H and prior has lower expected error, and it can output a label no single hypothesis in H predicts. It is also usually intractable, which is the whole motivation for approximations like Naive Bayes and for ensembles.

Two results that connect Bayes to the rest of the unit

Least squares is maximum likelihood

If y = f(x) + ε with ε ∼ N(0, σ²) i.i.d., then maximizing P(D|h) is exactly minimizing Σ(yi − h(xi))². Squared-error regression is not an arbitrary choice — it is the Gaussian-noise assumption written down. Similarly, an L2 weight penalty is a zero-mean Gaussian prior on the weights, so ridge/weight decay is MAP estimation.

MDL — minimum description length

Taking −log₂ of the MAP objective turns it into argminh [ length(h) + length(D | h) ]: bits to describe the hypothesis plus bits to describe the data given it. Occam's razor falls out of Bayes rule, and this is the principled version of "prefer short trees."

Naive Bayes

vNB = argmaxv ∈ V P(v) · Πi P(ai | v)the "naive" step: attributes conditionally independent given the class

The independence assumption is almost always false, yet the classifier is often excellent — because you only need the argmax to be right, not the probabilities. Its estimated probabilities are typically badly calibrated (pushed toward 0 and 1) even when its decisions are good; say that rather than reporting its confidences as if they were meaningful.

  • Cost: linear in data, one pass, O(nd); parameters grow as O(d·|V|) rather than exponentially — that is the entire payoff of the assumption.
  • Zero-frequency problem: one unseen attribute–class pair sends the whole product to zero. Fix with m-estimate / Laplace smoothing: (nc + mp)/(n + m).
  • Handles missing attributes gracefully (drop the factor), and is a strong text-classification baseline.

Unit 2 · assignment 2 & the RO report

Randomized optimization

Find argmaxx ∈ X f(x) when you have no derivative, no convexity, and only the ability to evaluate f. Four algorithms, and a story about what each one remembers.

The framing: what each algorithm keeps

The cleanest way to order this unit is by memory. Every step up the ladder retains more information about the search so far, and pays for it in evaluations per iteration.

AlgorithmWhat it retainsPer-iteration costWins when
Random hill climbingOne pointCheapestUnimodal, or basins are wide and easy to fall into
Simulated annealingOne point + a temperatureCheapRugged landscape, single good basin, plenty of iterations
Genetic algorithmA populationModerateSolution decomposes into recombinable building blocks
MIMICA probability distribution over good pointsExpensive per iterationEvaluating f is expensive; structure/dependencies matter

The vocabulary you should use precisely: fitness function f, neighbourhood N(x) (which points count as "one step away" — you choose this, and it determines the landscape's ruggedness as much as f does), basin of attraction, local optimum vs global optimum, and function evaluations as the currency. Compare algorithms by wall clock and by fitness evaluations; they rank differently, and noticing that is a report-grade observation.

No free lunch

Averaged over all possible fitness functions, every search algorithm has identical expected performance. So "algorithm A is better" is meaningless without "…on problems with structure S." Every claim in your RO report should name the structure: deceptiveness, plateaus, decomposability, basin width.

Hill climbing & random restarts

RHC(f, N): x ← random point loop: x′ ← argmaxn ∈ N(x) f(n) # or a random neighbour, for stochastic HC if f(x′) ≤ f(x): return x # local optimum x ← x′ Random-restart HC: run RHC k times from fresh random starts, keep the best.

Pure hill climbing is greedy and gets stuck at the first local optimum, on plateaus (no improving neighbour but not a peak) and on ridges (progress requires moving diagonally, which the neighbourhood may not allow). Restarts convert the algorithm from "find the basin I landed in" to "sample basins": if a fraction p of random starts land in the global basin, the expected number of restarts to find it is 1/p, and the chance of missing it after k restarts is (1−p)k. That is why RHC with enough restarts is a genuinely strong baseline and why beating it is the bar in assignment 2.

Where points get lost

Reporting RHC results without stating the restart count and the total evaluation budget. RHC with 200 restarts and SA with 200× more iterations are not comparable to "SA with the default schedule" — fix the evaluation budget across algorithms and say you did.

Simulated annealing

Hill climbing that is allowed to go downhill, with a probability that decays over time. The metaphor is metallurgical: heat the metal so atoms rearrange freely, then cool slowly enough that they settle into a low-energy crystal instead of freezing into a defect.

SA(f, N, T₀, cooling α): x ← random point; T ← T₀ repeat until T < Tmin: x′ ← random neighbour in N(x) ΔE ← f(x′) − f(x) if ΔE > 0: x ← x′ # always accept improvement else: x ← x′ with prob. eΔE/T # sometimes accept a worse point T ← α·T # geometric cooling, α ∈ (0,1)
P(accept a worse move) = exp(ΔE / T)T → ∞: accept anything — a random walk.   T → 0: accept nothing worse — pure hill climbing.

The two results to quote

  • With a sufficiently slow cooling schedule (logarithmic, Tt ∝ 1/log t), SA converges to the global optimum with probability 1. This is a theoretical guarantee, not a practical schedule — geometric cooling with α ≈ 0.95–0.999 is what you actually run, and you should note the gap.
  • At a fixed temperature the stationary distribution is Boltzmann: π(x) ∝ ef(x)/T. High T flattens it toward uniform; low T concentrates it on the optima. This is the single most quotable fact about SA and it also explains softmax exploration in Unit 4.
Tuning, in report language

T₀ should be high enough that early acceptance of worse moves is common (a rule of thumb: set T₀ so ~80% of uphill-and-downhill moves are accepted initially, which you can estimate from the spread of ΔE in a random walk). α controls the exploration/exploitation trade: cool too fast and SA degenerates into hill climbing from one random start; cool too slowly and you burn the whole budget on a random walk.

Genetic algorithms

Keep a population instead of a point, and generate new candidates by recombining good ones. Crossover is the only genuinely new idea in the unit — it is a jump through the space that no local search can make.

GA(f, pop size P, mating pool m, mutation rate μ): population ← P random individuals repeat: evaluate fitness of all individuals select m parents # fitness-proportional (roulette), tournament, or truncation offspring ← crossover(pairs of parents) mutate offspring with rate μ population ← replace (generational, or elitist: keep the best k) until converged
OperatorVariantEffect
SelectionRoulette (fitness-proportional)Fast convergence; risks a super-individual taking over → premature convergence
TournamentPick k at random, take the best; k tunes selection pressure smoothly

Building blocks and deception

The intuition is the building-block hypothesis: short, low-order, high-fitness schemata (partial patterns like 1 1 * * * 0) are combined by crossover into better full solutions. That works when the problem is decomposable. A deceptive problem is one where the low-order building blocks point away from the global optimum — and there GA does poorly while something that models dependencies (MIMIC) or that can traverse the whole path (SA with enough patience) does better.

Where points get lost

Attributing GA success to mutation. Mutation alone plus selection is essentially parallel hill climbing. The claim to test — and to report on — is whether crossover is doing the work: run the same GA with crossover disabled and compare. That single ablation is worth more than another hyper-parameter sweep.

MIMIC

Mutual-Information-Maximizing Input Clustering. Instead of carrying points, carry a probability distribution over good points, and refine it. It is the only algorithm in the unit that conveys structure between iterations.

MIMIC(f, sample size N, keep quantile q): θ₀ ← uniform over X repeat: sample N points from Pθt(x) # currently: P(x | f(x) ≥ θ_t) θt+1 ← the q-th percentile fitness of that sample # raise the bar keep the points with f(x) ≥ θt+1 re-estimate the distribution from those survivors until θ stops rising

The estimated distribution is a dependency tree: MIMIC approximates the true joint by a chain/tree of pairwise conditionals and picks the tree that minimizes KL divergence to the truth, which is equivalent to a maximum-spanning-tree over pairwise mutual information (the Chow–Liu construction). That is why the algorithm's name mentions mutual information, and it is the link back to Unit 0.

Why it wins
  • Orders of magnitude fewer fitness evaluations — decisive when f is a simulation or a physical experiment.
  • It represents dependencies between variables, so it handles structured and deceptive problems the GA's blind crossover cannot.
  • The learned tree is itself interpretable output — you can read which variables interact.
Why it often loses in practice
  • Each iteration costs far more compute (sampling + estimating a tree over d² mutual informations).
  • Needs a large enough sample per iteration or the structure estimate is noise.
  • Wall-clock comparisons flatter the cheap algorithms; evaluation-count comparisons flatter MIMIC. Report both.

Unit 3 · assignment 3 & the UL report

Unsupervised learning & dimensionality reduction

No labels. Two jobs: group the data (clustering) and shrink the description (feature selection and transformation) — then show what each does to a supervised learner downstream.

k-means (Lloyd's algorithm)

k-means(X, k): initialize k centers # random points, or k-means++ repeat: assign: each x → nearest center (Voronoi partition) update: each center → mean of its assigned points until assignments stop changing

It is coordinate descent on one objective — within-cluster sum of squares:

J = Σj=1..k Σx ∈ Cj ‖x − μj‖²The assign step minimizes J over assignments with centers fixed; the update step minimizes J over centers with assignments fixed

Why it converges — the proof you should be able to sketch

Each step weakly decreases J (the mean is the unique minimizer of squared distance to a set of points, and reassigning to the nearest center cannot increase it), J ≥ 0 is bounded below, and there are finitely many possible partitions (kn), so it cannot cycle and must terminate — in finite time, at a local optimum. It emphatically does not find the global optimum; the global k-means problem is NP-hard.

The properties and failure modes

  • Assumes spherical, similar-sized, similar-density clusters, because the only geometry is Euclidean distance to a mean. It fails on elongated, nested or crescent-shaped clusters.
  • Sensitive to initialization — run it many times with different seeds and keep the lowest J; or use k-means++, which seeds centers with probability proportional to squared distance from the nearest existing center and comes with an O(log k) approximation guarantee in expectation.
  • Sensitive to scale and to outliers (a mean is not robust; k-medoids is).
  • Complexity O(n·k·d·i) per run.
  • Choosing k: elbow on J, silhouette score (mean of (b−a)/max(a,b) — cohesion vs separation, range −1..1), gap statistic, or — in this course's assignment — downstream supervised performance and label agreement measures like adjusted Rand index or normalized mutual information.

Generalize the idea

EM is not about Gaussians. It is the general recipe for maximum likelihood with hidden variables: guess the hidden values in expectation (E), maximize the parameters as if that guess were data (M), repeat. It reappears in HMMs (Baum–Welch) and in POMDP belief updates.

Hierarchical clustering & the impossibility theorem

Single-linkage agglomerative clustering: start with n singleton clusters, repeatedly merge the two clusters whose closest pair of points is nearest, until k remain. Complexity O(n³) naively, O(n² log n) with a heap. The linkage choice changes everything:

Kleinberg's impossibility theorem

No clustering function on distances can satisfy all three of these simultaneously:

  • Richness — every possible partition of the points is achievable for some distance function.
  • Scale invariance — multiplying all distances by c > 0 leaves the clustering unchanged.
  • Consistency — shrinking within-cluster distances and stretching between-cluster distances leaves the clustering unchanged.

Each pair is achievable; all three are not. The practical reading, and the one to write down: clustering has no objectively correct answer. Every algorithm silently gives one of the three up (fixing k sacrifices richness; a fixed distance threshold sacrifices scale invariance), so a clustering result must be justified by what you do with it, not by the algorithm's authority.

PCA, mechanically

1. center X (and usually standardize — otherwise the largest-unit feature wins) 2. C = XᵀX / (n−1) # covariance 3. eigendecompose C = VΛVᵀ # or take the SVD of X directly 4. keep the top m eigenvectors by eigenvalue 5. Z = X·Vm # projected data explained variance ratio of component i = λi / Σλ

Facts worth memorizing: eigenvalues are the variance along each component, they are non-negative, they sum to the total variance; the first PC is the direction of maximum variance and also the best-fitting line in a least-squares-perpendicular sense; components are mutually orthogonal; a zero eigenvalue means a linearly dependent direction. PCA is the optimal linear transformation for L2 reconstruction — which is exactly why it can destroy a discriminative signal that happens to live along a low-variance direction.

Where points get lost

Assuming high variance means high usefulness. PCA is unsupervised — it has never seen your labels. If the class-separating direction has small variance, PCA will discard it and downstream accuracy drops. Report that when it happens; it is a finding, not a failure.

ICA in one paragraph

ICA assumes observations are linear mixtures of statistically independent, non-Gaussian sources and recovers the unmixing matrix by maximizing non-Gaussianity (kurtosis or negentropy). It cannot recover Gaussian sources — a mixture of Gaussians is Gaussian and the rotation is unidentifiable — and it recovers sources only up to permutation and scale. On images, PCA components look like blurry global averages; ICA components look like edges. On a tabular dataset, ICA components with high kurtosis are the interesting ones.

Unit 4 · assignment 4 & the RL report

Reinforcement learning

Decisions over time, with delayed and scalar feedback. The MDP is the model; Bellman is the equation; everything else is how you solve it with or without knowing the model.

Markov decision processes

An MDP is the tuple ⟨S, A, T, R, γ⟩: states, actions, transition model T(s,a,s′) = P(s′ | s,a), reward R(s) (or R(s,a) / R(s,a,s′)), and discount γ ∈ [0,1).

The Markov property: the next state depends only on the current state and action, not on history. "Only the present matters" — and if it does not hold, you fix it by enlarging the state, not by ignoring it. Also assume stationarity: T and R do not change over time.

Return:   Gt = Rt + γRt+1 + γ²Rt+2 + … = Σk≥0 γkRt+kWith |R| ≤ Rmax, the infinite sum is bounded by Rmax/(1−γ) — that is why γ < 1 makes infinite horizons well-defined

What γ means: γ → 0 is myopic (only immediate reward); γ → 1 is far-sighted and mathematically slower to converge. A useful reading is that 1/(1−γ) is the effective horizon — γ = 0.9 ≈ 10 steps, γ = 0.99 ≈ 100 steps. In a gridworld with a small negative step cost, lowering γ or making the step cost more negative pushes the optimal policy toward short, risky routes; a positive step reward can make the optimal policy avoid terminating at all.

Where points get lost

Confusing a policy π : S → A (what to do everywhere, not just along one path) with a plan (a fixed action sequence). Under stochastic transitions a plan is useless after the first surprise — that is precisely why RL learns policies.

Bellman equations, value iteration, policy iteration

Vπ(s) = R(s) + γ Σs′ T(s,π(s),s′) Vπ(s′)
V*(s) = R(s) + γ maxa Σs′ T(s,a,s′) V*(s′)

Value iteration
V₀ ← arbitrary repeat: Vk+1(s) ← R(s) + γ maxa Σ T(s,a,s′)Vk(s′) until maxs|Vk+1−Vk| < ε π(s) = greedy w.r.t. V

Cost per sweep O(|S|²|A|). Converges geometrically; the policy is usually optimal long before the values are.

Policy iteration
π₀ ← arbitrary repeat: evaluate: solve Vπ (linear system, |S| equations, or iterate to converge) improve: π′(s) ← argmaxa Σ T·Vπ until π′ = π

Evaluation costs O(|S|³) if solved exactly, but there are finitely many policies and each iteration strictly improves, so it converges in few iterations — often faster in practice.

Why it converges: the contraction argument

The Bellman operator B is a γ-contraction in the max norm: ‖BV − BU‖∞ ≤ γ‖V − U‖∞. By the Banach fixed-point theorem it has a unique fixed point (V*) and repeated application converges to it from any start, with error shrinking by a factor γ per sweep. Being able to state this — contraction, unique fixed point, geometric convergence, independent of initialization — is the highest-value fact in Unit 4.

Q-learning & temporal difference

Drop the model. Q-learning learns Q* from experience tuples (s, a, r, s′) alone — no T, no R, and it still converges to the optimal Q.

The convergence conditions — quote all three

  1. Every state–action pair is visited infinitely often (so exploration must never fully stop).
  2. The learning rate satisfies the Robbins–Monro conditions: Σαt = ∞ and Σαt² < ∞ — e.g. αt = 1/t. A constant α violates the second and gives you a bounded oscillation instead of convergence, which is often what you want in a non-stationary problem.
  3. Finite MDP with bounded rewards.

The TD family

TD(0) updates from one step: V(s) ← V(s) + α[r + γV(s′) − V(s)] — low variance, biased by the current estimate (bootstrapping). Monte Carlo waits for the full return: unbiased, high variance, needs episodes to terminate. TD(λ) interpolates with eligibility traces: λ = 0 is TD(0), λ = 1 is (every-visit) Monte Carlo, and intermediate λ ≈ 0.7–0.9 is usually best because it spreads credit backward over the whole recent trajectory instead of one step at a time.

Bootstrapping, in one sentence

"TD methods update a guess toward a guess." That is the source of both their efficiency (learn from incomplete episodes, online) and their bias.

Exploration vs exploitation

You cannot learn about actions you never take, and you cannot collect reward while experimenting. Everything in this section is a way of pricing that trade.

The bandit case (one state) is where the theory is clean: regret is the cumulative gap to the best arm, and the best algorithms achieve O(log t) regret. RL adds the complication that exploring changes which states you even reach.

Reward shaping

Adding intermediate rewards speeds learning but can change the optimal policy — the classic failure is an agent that farms the shaping reward in a loop instead of finishing. Potential-based shaping is the safe form: F(s,s′) = γΦ(s′) − Φ(s) for any potential function Φ. It provably leaves the optimal policy unchanged, and naming it is worth a mark whenever shaping comes up.

Three ideas that round out the unit:

  • Function approximation. Tabular Q needs a cell per state–action pair, so it dies on large or continuous state spaces. Replace the table with Q(s,a;θ) — linear features or a neural net. The cost is that convergence guarantees weaken: the "deadly triad" of bootstrapping + off-policy + function approximation can diverge.
  • POMDPs. When the state is not observable, act on a belief state — a probability distribution over states, updated by Bayes rule from observations. The belief MDP is a continuous-state MDP, and solving it exactly is intractable in general.
  • Game theory / multi-agent. When another learner shares the environment it is no longer stationary. Know: a Nash equilibrium is a joint strategy where no player gains by unilateral deviation; every finite game has one in mixed strategies; two-player zero-sum games are solved by minimax (and the minimax value equals the Nash value); the prisoner's dilemma shows the Nash equilibrium can be Pareto-dominated; and repeated play with the folly of a fixed horizon unravels cooperation by backward induction while an indefinite horizon (via γ) can sustain it — the folk theorem.

Self-test: the questions that actually get asked

You add 10,000 more training examples and CV error does not move. What do you conclude, and what do you do?

High bias, not high variance — variance error shrinks with data and bias does not. The fix is a richer hypothesis space or better features: deeper tree, more hidden units, a nonlinear kernel, feature engineering. Adding data or regularizing further is wasted effort.

Boosting has driven training error to zero at round 40, yet test error is still falling at round 300. Explain.

Further rounds keep increasing the margin — the confidence with which points are classified — even though the sign of every prediction is already right. Generalization bounds for voted classifiers depend on the margin distribution rather than on the number of rounds, so test error can keep improving. Caveat to state in the same answer: with label noise this reverses, because AdaBoost's exponential reweighting pours unbounded weight onto mislabelled points.

Why is SA with a very high fixed temperature useless, and with a very low one also useless?

The acceptance probability is eΔE/T. As T → ∞ that tends to 1 for every move, so the search is an unbiased random walk that never exploits. As T → 0 it tends to 0 for every worsening move, so SA reduces to hill climbing and gets trapped in the first local optimum. The value is entirely in the schedule between the two — and the stationary distribution π(x) ∝ ef(x)/T shows exactly how the concentration on optima tightens as T falls.

A GA and a random-restart hill climber both solve Four Peaks, but the GA needs far fewer evaluations. Why?

Four Peaks rewards combining a prefix of 1s with a suffix of 0s. Each half is a separate building block that local search can discover, but a hill climber holding one half cannot acquire the other without descending through a large fitness valley. Crossover splices two parents that each solved one half, reaching the global optimum in a single operation. The general claim: GA beats local search when the problem decomposes into recombinable, independently-discoverable blocks.

PCA reduced your data to 10 components capturing 95% of variance, and your classifier got worse. Is PCA broken?

No — it did what it optimizes. PCA is unsupervised and maximizes retained variance, which is not the same as retained discriminability. If the class-separating direction has small variance (a subtle but consistent offset), it lands in the discarded 5%. Remedies: LDA (supervised), keep more components, or use a selection method scored on the actual learner. Reporting this as a finding — with the scree plot and the accuracy drop side by side — is a stronger result than a small accuracy gain.

Why does Q-learning converge to π* even when the agent behaves randomly the whole time?

Because the update target uses maxa′ Q(s′,a′), not the action the behaviour policy will actually take — it is off-policy. The behaviour policy only has to guarantee that every state–action pair is visited infinitely often; the learned values are those of the greedy policy regardless. With the Robbins–Monro learning-rate conditions and a finite MDP, Q converges to Q* with probability 1. Contrast SARSA, whose target uses the action actually taken, so it converges to the value of the exploring policy.

State the VC dimension of linear separators in ℝ² and prove it.

It is 3. Lower bound: three points in general position (a triangle) can be labelled in all 8 ways by some line — exhibit the cases. Upper bound: no set of 4 points can be shattered. Either one point lies inside the triangle of the other three (label it opposite to the rest and no line works), or the four form a convex quadrilateral (label the two diagonal pairs oppositely — XOR — and no line separates them). Since VC = d + 1 for ℝd, this generalizes.

Your k-means silhouette score peaks at k = 2, but the data has 7 true classes. What went wrong?

Probably nothing algorithmic. Silhouette measures geometric cohesion and separation under a Euclidean metric — it finds the structure the metric can see, which need not match semantic labels. Likely culprits: unscaled features letting one dominate distance; non-spherical or unequal-density classes violating k-means' assumptions; several true classes overlapping in feature space. Diagnose by scoring cluster–label agreement (adjusted Rand, NMI) as well as silhouette, and by re-clustering after PCA/ICA. Kleinberg's theorem is the honest framing: there is no k that is correct independent of what you want the clustering for.

When would you prefer policy iteration over value iteration, and vice versa?

Policy iteration when |S| is small enough that exact policy evaluation (an O(|S|³) linear solve, or a cheap iterative one) is affordable — it converges in very few iterations because the policy space is finite and each step strictly improves. Value iteration when the state space is large: each sweep is only O(|S|²|A|), and you can stop early since the greedy policy typically becomes optimal well before the values converge. Modified policy iteration (a few evaluation sweeps rather than an exact solve) is the usual compromise, and mentioning it shows you understand they are two ends of one dial.

Give the one-line inductive bias for each of: decision trees, k-NN, neural nets, SVMs, Naive Bayes.

Trees: prefer short trees with informative attributes near the root, axis-parallel splits. k-NN: nearby points share labels, all features weigh equally in the metric. Neural nets: nearly unrestricted representation, but gradient descent from small weights prefers smooth, low-complexity functions. SVM: among separators, prefer the maximum-margin one; the kernel fixes the space it lives in. Naive Bayes: the MAP hypothesis under the assumption that attributes are conditionally independent given the class.

Twelve traps that cost marks

  1. Reporting accuracy on imbalanced data.
  2. Tuning on the test set — or plotting test error vs hyper-parameter and calling it validation.
  3. Not standardizing features before k-NN, SVM-RBF, neural nets, k-means or PCA.
  4. Calling k-NN "unbiased".
  5. Claiming backprop or k-means or EM finds a global optimum.
  6. Saying boosting never overfits, without the noise caveat.
  7. Treating KL divergence as a distance.
  8. Confusing "shatters some set of d points" with "shatters every set of d points".
  9. Comparing RO algorithms without fixing the evaluation budget, or reporting only wall clock.
  10. Assuming PCA's high-variance components are the useful ones.
  11. Applying ICA to Gaussian sources and expecting recovery.
  12. Comparing Q-learning to value iteration on episode count while ignoring that one has the model and the other does not.
